* Report Rubric

1. *Introduction (10 pts)*
   - Give a brief background of the research problem and description of the study design.
   - Clearly outline the question(s) of interest you will address with the statistical analysis. The more specific you define the question of interest here, the easier the rest of the analysis is.
2. *Statistical Procedures Used (10 pts)*
   - This section should lay out the steps, decisions, and logic for making those decisions leading to the final model used to address questions of interest. The following should be included, and should be written up in a coherent report format.
   - Describe the response variable and explanatory variables, the details of the study design (concept diagrams can be helpful here) identifying any design variables and describing the measurement structure of the data (if applicable).
   - Describe the theoretical statistical model you will use to address each research question, identifying which parameters allow you to address the research question (define parameters of interest in words).
   - Conduct EDA (exploratory data analysis) and create appropriate plots of the raw data. Describe what you learn from the plots and include figure captions for your plots.
   - Describe any statistical procedures you will use to make inference related to the research question (e.g. two-sample t-test, analysis of variance, extra sum-of-squares F-test, etc). Be specific about which procedures (and parameters in models) are addressing which questions of interest (if there are multiple questions).
   - Describe how you assessed assumptions, what your conclusions regarding the assumptions are, and justify your conclusions. Refer to the appropriate plots and include pertinent plots that are in addition to EDA plots (QQ-plots, residual plots, etc.)
3. *Summary of Statistical Findings (10 pts)*
   - Write up your /Summary of Statistical Findings/ for each question of interest. Follow examples in /The Sleuth/ and the information in /Writing a Summary of Statistical Findings/ handout.
   - You can usually do this in two sentences: (1) Interpret the $p$-value in an "evidence" sentence and (2) provide and interpret point estimates and confidence intervals in the "it is estimated" sentence.
   - Do NOT use the word /significant/ (the s-word) in any of its forms. Replace the s-word with words describing what you actually mean by it. Also try to avoid using the word /determine/ in the contexts of addressing questions of interest because they can never be definitively answered. Only use the word /correlation/ if you are actually calculating one!
4. *Conclusion in context (i.e., with appropriate Scope of Inference) (10 pts)*
   - Write a brief /Conclusion statement that includes the appropriate Scope of Inference/. See examples given in /The Sleuth/ and in the /Scope of Inference writing examples/ handout. Specifically, answer these two questions and comment on their implications:
     1. Were the units randomly selected from some larger population? (i.e. What, if any, larger population can you infer the results to?)
     2. Were the units randomly assigned to groups? (i.e. Are cause-and-effect statements justified?)
   - Make sure you write the /Scope of Inference/ specific to the language of the problem (/not/ just generic statements). For example, DO NOT say "There was no randomization so cause and effect statements cannot be made." Instead, for example, say "The lizards were not randomly assigned to diets, therefore we can not conclude that the fly diet caused any increase in average weight over the lizards on the tofu diet."

* My draft conclusion for the Part II report


There is evidence that an interaction between arm-position and hand-dominance is not likely. There does appear to be a negative impact on grip strength when testing their non-dominant hand compared to using their dominant hand. Similarly, testing grip strength with the user's arm-position to the side performed better than testing grip strength with the user's arm-position at a 90-degree angle. 
The students were randomly assigned an order to test grip strength between the combinations of hand-dominance and arm-position, therefore we can conclude that there is a causal relationship between grip strength and combination of hand-dominance and arm-position.
The people selected for gathering the dataset were a convenient sample of STATX12 students instead of a random selection, therefore we can only project these findings to Montana State University students in the spring 2026 semester taking STATX12 (assuming this is the only STATX12 class).
