Putting a learner into a working experiment: baselines, preparing features,
honest evaluation, and the traps in real data. Every question states its own
scenario. Units =proj1= and =proj2=. In the pseudocode answers, leading dots
mark indentation depth.

* Baselines

** Before fitting any model, you want a baseline that ignores the features entirely. What should it predict for classification, and for regression?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: The most frequent class label, and the mean of the responses. Neither looks at a single feature value.
:Q_UNIT: proj1
:END:

** A baseline that ignores every feature seems useless. Give two distinct reasons to build one anyway.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: It is the floor any real model must clear. And it exercises the entire pipeline -- loading, splitting, scoring -- without depending on the model being correct.
:Q_UNIT: proj1
:END:

** Your k-NN classifier scores no better than a model that always predicts the most common class. What are the possible explanations?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: The features carry no signal for this target; the distance is broken by unnormalized or mis-encoded features; the hyperparameters are badly tuned; or the pipeline has a bug.
:Q_UNIT: proj1
:END:

** A null model for a classification problem predicts the __________ class for every input.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: fill
:Q_ANSWER: majority
:Q_ACCEPT: most common | most frequent | plurality
:Q_EXPLAIN: For regression it predicts the mean (or median) of the training targets. Either way it ignores the features entirely.
:Q_UNIT: proj1
:END:

** The main reason to build a null model is that it:
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: mc
:Q_OPTIONS: establishes the error any real model must beat | trains quickly | validates the data loader | reduces variance
:Q_ANSWER: establishes the error any real model must beat
:Q_EXPLAIN: It also catches pipeline bugs: a real model scoring at or below the null is evidence of a broken feature path, not of a hard problem.
:Q_UNIT: proj1
:END:

* Preparing Features for a Distance

** Explain why features must be normalized before you compute a distance between examples.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: The distance sums each feature's contribution, so a feature measured in thousands swamps one measured in tenths. Normalizing gives each comparable influence.
:Q_UNIT: proj1
:END:

** Explain min-max normalization and its weakness.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Rescale linearly so the observed range maps onto a fixed interval. One extreme outlier stretches the range and squeezes every other value into a narrow band.
:Q_UNIT: proj1
:END:

** Explain z-score normalization and its weakness.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Subtract the mean and divide by the standard deviation, making features comparable in units of spread. The result is unbounded, so extreme values stay extreme.
:Q_UNIT: proj1
:END:

** Compare min-max and z-score normalization. When would you choose each?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Min-max suits already-bounded features and puts everything on one scale. Z-score suits skewed or outlier-heavy data, where min-max would compress the bulk of the values.
:Q_UNIT: proj1
:END:

** You are running cross-validation. On which portion of the data must the normalization constants be computed, and what goes wrong otherwise?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: On the training portion only, then applied to the test portion. Computing them over everything leaks test information into training and biases your reported error optimistically.
:Q_UNIT: proj1
:END:

** Normalization constants must come from the training portion alone. What other quantities in a nearest-neighbor pipeline carry the same leakage risk?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Anything fitted from data: the class counts behind a categorical distance, the tuned hyperparameters, and any reduced exemplar set. All must come from the training portion.
:Q_UNIT: proj1
:END:

** Explain the idea behind the Value Difference Metric for measuring distance between categorical values.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Two values are close when the examples carrying them have the same class distribution. Distance is measured through the evidence a value gives about the label.
:Q_UNIT: proj1
:END:

** Why can the Value Difference Metric not be used on a regression problem, and what would you use instead?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: It is built from class counts, and regression has no classes. Use one-hot encoding with Hamming distance instead, accepting the loss of any ordering.
:Q_UNIT: proj1
:END:

** Your data has both numeric and categorical features. Explain how to produce a single distance between two examples.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Compute a numeric distance and a categorical distance separately using the same exponent, omit the final root from both, and add them together.
:Q_UNIT: proj1
:END:

** When combining a numeric and a categorical distance, you omit the final pth root from both. Why is that safe?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: The root is monotone, so it cannot change which neighbors come out nearest -- and the ordering is all a nearest-neighbor method uses.
:Q_UNIT: proj1
:END:

** You combined two distances without taking the pth root. What must you then avoid doing with the resulting numbers?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Treating them as true distances. Anything expecting a real metric -- a kernel assuming Euclidean distance, say -- will be scaled wrongly. Use them only to rank neighbors.
:Q_UNIT: proj1
:END:

** A feature encodes the month of the year as the integers 1 through 12. Explain what goes wrong when you use it in an ordinary distance.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: December and January come out 11 apart when they are adjacent. The feature is cyclic, so a linear encoding misrepresents its geometry.
:Q_UNIT: proj1
:END:

** Normalization constants must be computed from the __________ portion of the data alone.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: fill
:Q_ANSWER: training
:Q_ACCEPT: training portion | train
:Q_EXPLAIN: Computing a min, max, mean or standard deviation over all the data lets the test fold influence the transform applied to it -- leakage, and an optimistic error.
:Q_UNIT: proj1
:END:

** Which normalization is the more disturbed by a single extreme outlier?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: mc
:Q_OPTIONS: min-max | z-score | neither | both equally
:Q_ANSWER: min-max
:Q_EXPLAIN: One extreme value sets the range and crushes every other example into a narrow band. A z-score is affected through the mean and standard deviation, but far less sharply.
:Q_UNIT: proj1
:END:

** Features must be normalized before a distance is computed because otherwise:
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: mc
:Q_OPTIONS: the feature with the widest numeric range dominates the distance | the distance can be negative | the metric axioms fail | categorical features cannot be mixed in
:Q_ANSWER: the feature with the widest numeric range dominates the distance
:Q_EXPLAIN: Which makes the choice of units an accidental form of feature weighting -- income in dollars would outvote age entirely.
:Q_UNIT: proj1
:END:

** A month encoded as the integers 1 through 12 is safe to use directly in a distance.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: tf
:Q_ANSWER: False
:Q_EXPLAIN: It is cyclic: December and January are adjacent in reality but maximally far apart as integers. Encode it as a pair of sine and cosine components instead.
:Q_UNIT: proj1
:END:

** When combining a numeric and a categorical distance, dropping the final pth root from both is safe because:
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: mc
:Q_OPTIONS: the root is monotone, so neighbor ordering is unchanged | the root is always 1 | the distances are already normalized | the two distances have the same scale
:Q_ANSWER: the root is monotone, so neighbor ordering is unchanged
:Q_EXPLAIN: Nearest-neighbor search only needs the ranking. What you must then avoid is treating the resulting numbers as distances -- no kernel bandwidth, no threshold, no reporting them.
:Q_UNIT: proj1
:END:

* Tuning and Reducing a Nearest-Neighbor Model

** Explain how a k-nearest-neighbor regressor turns its neighbors into a single prediction.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: A kernel-weighted mean of their responses, so nearer neighbors count more. The bandwidth controls how quickly that weighting decays with distance.
:Q_UNIT: proj1
:END:

** Explain what happens to a kernel-weighted k-NN regressor as the bandwidth goes to each extreme.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Very narrow: only the closest neighbor matters, so it becomes 1-NN -- low bias, high variance. Very wide: the weights equalize toward a plain mean -- high bias, low variance.
:Q_UNIT: proj1
:END:

** Edited and condensed nearest neighbor both run with k = 1, while prediction uses a separately tuned k. Explain why the two values differ.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Reduction asks whether a single nearest stored point suffices, which is a question about one neighbor. Prediction asks how many neighbors give the best vote -- a different question.
:Q_UNIT: proj1
:END:

** You are applying edited or condensed nearest neighbor to a regression problem. What extra hyperparameter do you need, and why?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: An error threshold. Both methods test whether a prediction disagrees with the truth, and continuous responses are never simply unequal, so a tolerance has to define disagreement.
:Q_UNIT: proj1
:END:

** Describe how to tune k for a nearest-neighbor model without contaminating your final error estimate.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Split the training portion again, sweep \(k\) on that inner validation part, refit on the full training portion with the winner, and score once on the untouched test portion.
:Q_UNIT: proj1
:END:

** Describe the end-to-end procedure for evaluating a nearest-neighbor model honestly, from raw data to a reported error.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Every quantity fitted from data is fitted inside the training half; the test half is touched once, at scoring.
:Q_STEPS: drop identifier features; apply any target transform | for each of 5 repetitions: | .  shuffle and split the data into halves A and B | .  for (train, test) in [ (A,B), (B,A) ]: | .  .  fit normalization on train only, apply to both | .  .  build categorical distance tables from train only | .  .  split train again and sweep hyperparameters on that inner split | .  .  refit on the full train half with the chosen values | .  .  build any reduced exemplar set from train only | .  .  score the baseline and the models on test | report the distribution of the ten scores per model
:Q_UNIT: proj1
:END:

** Editing and condensing run with k = 1 while prediction uses a tuned k because:
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: mc
:Q_OPTIONS: the reduction is about reproducing the 1-NN boundary | k = 1 is faster | a tuned k is unavailable at that stage | the two values must differ
:Q_ANSWER: the reduction is about reproducing the 1-NN boundary
:Q_EXPLAIN: They are asking which stored points are needed to preserve the boundary; prediction is a separate question about how much smoothing the data wants.
:Q_UNIT: proj1
:END:

** Applying edited or condensed nearest neighbor to a regression problem requires one extra hyperparameter: an error __________.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: fill
:Q_ANSWER: threshold
:Q_ACCEPT: tolerance | epsilon | threshold (tolerance)
:Q_EXPLAIN: With a continuous target there is no "misclassified" to test, so you need a tolerance that says when a prediction counts as close enough.
:Q_UNIT: proj1
:END:

** As the bandwidth of a kernel-weighted k-NN regressor grows very large, its prediction approaches:
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: mc
:Q_OPTIONS: the mean of the training targets | the nearest neighbor's target | zero | the median
:Q_ANSWER: the mean of the training targets
:Q_EXPLAIN: Every neighbor gets near-equal weight, so it collapses to the null model. A very small bandwidth does the opposite and follows the single nearest point.
:Q_UNIT: proj1
:END:

* Traps in Real Data

** A column in your dataset holds a unique identifier for each row. What must you do with it, and why?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Discard it. It carries no generalizable information, and a tree will split on it to produce a perfect, useless lookup table.
:Q_UNIT: proj1
:END:

** A dataset encodes its two classes as the numbers 2 and 4. What mistake does that invite?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Treating them as quantities. They are labels, so averaging them or measuring the distance between them is meaningless.
:Q_UNIT: proj1
:END:

** A voting dataset records some entries as "?", and the documentation says this means the member abstained. Why would imputing those entries be wrong?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: An abstention is a real, informative third value, not missing data. Imputing it replaces a genuine signal with a guess about something that was never absent.
:Q_UNIT: proj1
:END:

** A dataset's target is an integer count taking 29 distinct values. Explain why treating it as a 29-class classification problem is wrong.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: The values are ordered, and regression exploits that -- being off by one is nearly right. Classification treats 5 and 6 as exactly as wrong as 5 and 28.
:Q_UNIT: proj1
:END:

** One of your features was produced by someone else fitting a regression to the very target you are predicting. What must you do with it, and why?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Remove it from the feature set. It is a near-perfect predictor derived from the answer, so it leaks the target -- your model would look excellent while learning nothing.
:Q_UNIT: proj1
:END:

** You removed a feature because it leaked the target. Is it now worthless, or is there still a use for it?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Still useful, as a benchmark. Keep it aside and compare your own predictions against it to see how close you come to that existing method.
:Q_UNIT: proj1
:END:

** A regression target is heavily concentrated at zero with a few very large values. Explain the problem this causes and the standard fix.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Squared error is dominated by the few extremes, dragging the model toward them. The fix is a log transform of the target, which compresses the long tail.
:Q_UNIT: proj1
:END:

** You are log-transforming a target whose values start at zero. Why add one before taking the logarithm?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: So that zero maps to zero rather than negative infinity. With the data concentrated at zero, that applies to most of it.
:Q_UNIT: proj1
:END:

** You log-transformed a regression target before fitting. What must you be careful about when reporting your error?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: State whether the error is in log space or back-transformed. The two are not comparable, and a good log-space error can hide large errors on the original scale.
:Q_UNIT: proj1
:END:

** A column holding a unique identifier for each row must be:
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: mc
:Q_OPTIONS: dropped before training | normalized | treated as categorical | used only in the tree
:Q_ANSWER: dropped before training
:Q_EXPLAIN: It carries no generalizable signal but has perfect apparent information gain, so a tree splits on it at the root and a distance is polluted by it.
:Q_UNIT: proj2
:END:

** In the Congressional Vote data, a "?" entry means the member abstained, so imputing it would:
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: mc
:Q_OPTIONS: invent a vote that was deliberately not cast | reduce the sample size | break the distance metric | only matter for the tree
:Q_ANSWER: invent a vote that was deliberately not cast
:Q_EXPLAIN: It is a real third category, not a missing value. Abstention is informative in itself and should be encoded as its own value.
:Q_UNIT: proj1
:END:

** A feature produced by someone else fitting a regression to the very target you are predicting must be dropped.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: tf
:Q_ANSWER: True
:Q_EXPLAIN: ERP on the Computer Hardware data. It leaks the target, so keeping it measures how well you can copy another model. It can still serve as a comparison benchmark, just not as an input.
:Q_UNIT: proj1
:END:

** Before log-transforming a target whose values start at zero, you add one because:
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: mc
:Q_OPTIONS: log 0 is undefined | it centres the data | it removes the skew | it normalizes the range
:Q_ANSWER: log 0 is undefined
:Q_EXPLAIN: log(y + 1) maps 0 to 0 and keeps the compression of the large values, which is the point of the transform on Forest Fires.
:Q_UNIT: proj1
:END:

* Building and Evaluating a Tree

** When a decision tree splits on a feature, what determines how many children the node gets?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: The feature's type. A categorical feature branches once per distinct value; a numeric feature gets a binary split on a threshold.
:Q_UNIT: proj2
:END:

** A decision tree handles categorical features without one-hot encoding, while k-nearest neighbor cannot. Explain why.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: A tree tests a feature's value directly and never computes a distance, so categories need no numeric form. k-NN must subtract feature values, which forces an encoding.
:Q_UNIT: proj2
:END:

** You are choosing a splitting criterion for a classification tree and want to avoid favouring many-valued features. What criterion, and how does it help?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Gain ratio -- information gain divided by intrinsic value. Dividing by the fragmentation cancels the advantage a feature gets purely from having many branches.
:Q_UNIT: proj2
:END:

** What splitting criterion does a regression tree use, and what is it minimizing?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Weighted mean squared error: for each candidate split, take each branch's squared deviation from its own mean, weight by branch size, and minimize the total.
:Q_UNIT: proj2
:END:

** You want to compare a fully grown decision tree against a pruned version of it. Describe how the data must be divided, and what each portion does.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Three disjoint roles. A portion held out up front decides the pruning; of the rest, each cross-validation fold's training part grows the tree and the held-out fold scores it.
:Q_UNIT: proj2
:END:

** Why must the data used to decide pruning be separate from the data the tree was grown on?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: On its own training data a fully grown tree is already optimal, so no prune would ever look acceptable and pruning would have no effect at all.
:Q_UNIT: proj2
:END:

** Why must the data used to decide pruning also be separate from the data you finally score on?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Pruning selects between models using that data, so it is a model-selection step. Scoring on it would report a number that had been tuned on the same examples.
:Q_UNIT: proj2
:END:

** Describe the end-to-end procedure for comparing a fully grown tree against a pruned one.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Hold out the pruning data once, cross-validate the rest, and score both trees on the same untouched fold.
:Q_STEPS: eliminate unique-identifier features | hold out a fixed fraction P, once, to decide pruning | for each of 5 repetitions over the remainder: | .  shuffle and split into halves A and B | .  for (train, test) in [ (A,B), (B,A) ]: | .  .  grow a tree on train, to completion | .  .  prune a COPY of it against P | .  .  score both trees on test | compare the two distributions of scores
:Q_EXPLAIN: Prune a copy -- you need the unpruned tree's score on the same fold for the comparison to mean anything.
:Q_UNIT: proj2
:END:

** You compare a fully grown tree against a pruned one. What hypothesis is that comparison testing, and what mechanism would make it true?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: That pruning improves generalization. A full tree has tiny high-variance leaves fitting noise; replacing them raises bias slightly and cuts variance substantially.
:Q_UNIT: proj2
:END:

** On what kind of dataset would you expect pruning to help a tree most, and why?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: One with weak features and a noisy target, where a fully grown tree has little real signal and so fits noise deeply. On nearly separable data there is far less to remove.
:Q_UNIT: proj2
:END:

** The splitting criterion that avoids favouring many-valued features is:
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: mc
:Q_OPTIONS: gain ratio | information gain | mean squared error | entropy
:Q_ANSWER: gain ratio
:Q_EXPLAIN: Dividing by intrinsic value penalizes a broad split, which is what stops a high-cardinality column winning the root on apparent gain alone.
:Q_UNIT: proj2
:END:

** A decision tree handles categorical features without one-hot encoding because:
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: mc
:Q_OPTIONS: a node can branch once per category | it normalizes internally | it converts them to integers | it ignores them
:Q_ANSWER: a node can branch once per category
:Q_EXPLAIN: It never needs a numeric distance between two categories, which is exactly what k-NN does need -- hence VDM.
:Q_UNIT: proj2
:END:

** In the project, the holdout reserved inside each training portion for pruning decisions is __________ percent.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: fill
:Q_ANSWER: 20
:Q_ACCEPT: 20% | twenty
:Q_EXPLAIN: It comes out of the training half of the 5x2 split, never out of the test half, so the reported error stays untouched by the pruning decisions.
:Q_UNIT: proj2
:END:

** Pruning helps a tree most on a dataset that is:
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: mc
:Q_OPTIONS: small or noisy | large and clean | high-dimensional | perfectly separable
:Q_ANSWER: small or noisy
:Q_EXPLAIN: That is where the deep branches are fitting noise rather than structure. On large clean data the full tree's extra splits are mostly earning their place.
:Q_UNIT: proj2
:END:

* Comparing the Two Approaches

** Compare how k-nearest neighbor and a decision tree each control overfitting, and name the hyperparameter responsible in each.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: k-NN smooths over neighbors, controlled by \(k\) and the kernel bandwidth. A tree prunes, controlled by the acceptance test against held-out data. Both reduce effective parameters.
:Q_UNIT: proj2
:END:

** Both a k-NN study and a pruned-tree study can use 5x2 cross-validation, but the tree study needs one extra holdout. Explain why, and where it comes from.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Pruning is a model-selection step needing data separate from both growing and scoring. So a portion is removed before cross-validation begins, giving three roles instead of two.
:Q_UNIT: proj2
:END:

** You must describe an algorithm precisely enough that someone could reimplement it, without showing any code. What has to be in that description?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: The criterion and its formula, the order of operations, where each hyperparameter enters and what it was tuned against, the stopping and tie-breaking rules, and the choices you made where more than one was valid.
:Q_UNIT: proj2
:END:

** The hyperparameter that controls overfitting is k for nearest neighbor and __________ for a decision tree.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: fill
:Q_ANSWER: tree depth
:Q_ACCEPT: depth | the pruning decision | tree size | depth (pruning)
:Q_EXPLAIN: Raising k and pruning a tree are the same move: both trade variance for bias by refusing to honour small pockets of the training data.
:Q_UNIT: proj2
:END:

** A pruned-tree study needs one holdout more than a k-NN study because:
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: mc
:Q_OPTIONS: pruning is a fitting decision that needs its own data | trees are slower | trees need more data | k-NN has no hyperparameters
:Q_ANSWER: pruning is a fitting decision that needs its own data
:Q_EXPLAIN: It must come from the training portion, separate both from the data the tree was grown on and from the test fold the result is reported on.
:Q_UNIT: proj2
:END:
