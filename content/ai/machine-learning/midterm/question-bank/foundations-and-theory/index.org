* What Learning Is

** What does it mean for a program to learn, as opposed to simply being programmed?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: It improves at a task with experience, measured against some criterion. Learning needs all three: a task, a performance measure, and experience that moves the measure.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** During learning, what is fixed in advance and what is actually adjusted?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: The model's form is fixed up to its parameters; only the parameters are optimized from data. Choosing that form is where inductive bias enters.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** Explain the difference between a predictive and a descriptive model.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: A predictive model forecasts future cases. A descriptive model explains structure already in the data. One model can do both.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** What is a discriminant?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: A function separating examples of different classes -- the boundary itself. In a threshold rule on income and savings, the two thresholds are the discriminant.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** Explain what an association rule captures and what it is used for.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: A conditional probability \(P(X \mid Y)\): given that a customer bought \(Y\), how likely is \(X\). Used for store layout and recommendation.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** Explain supervised learning in terms of who supplies what.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: A critic supplies the correct action, the learner converts it into a rule, and the actor applies that rule. The ground truth comes from outside the learner.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** Explain unsupervised learning, and why evaluating it is harder than evaluating a classifier.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Only inputs are given, and the learner finds structure unaided. There is no ground truth to score against, so you must judge the geometry of the result instead.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** Explain reinforcement learning and how its feedback differs from supervised learning.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: It solves sequential decision problems using a reinforcement signal -- a reward -- rather than the correct answer. It is told how good an action was, never what it should have done.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** What is a policy, and what is reinforcement learning actually trying to produce?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: A policy is the sequence of actions that reaches the goal. Learning the policy is the whole object of RL.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** Explain semi-supervised learning and the situation that motivates it.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: It mixes a little labeled data with a lot of unlabeled data, using approaches like self-training. It fits when labels are expensive but raw data is cheap.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** Compare online and offline learning.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Online, data arrives over time and the model updates as it comes. Offline, the data set is fixed up front and can be trained on at leisure.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** Explain active learning, and why it can need far fewer examples than passive learning.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: The learner chooses which instances to have labeled. Choosing a query that halves the hypothesis space extracts far more information than a randomly drawn example.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** Why is clustering not simply a form of classification?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Clustering finds geometric structure with no labels and no notion of a correct answer. Classification is a separate step layered on top, so classification metrics do not evaluate clusters.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** What distinguishes a classification problem from a regression problem?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: The output type: discrete class labels versus a continuous value. It changes the loss, the evaluation metric, and often the model.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

* The Winnow-2 Algorithm

** What kind of problem is Winnow-2 for, and what is the core idea behind how it learns?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Supervised binary classification from Boolean features. It keeps one weight per feature and learns a linear threshold, correcting the weights multiplicatively whenever -- and only when -- it predicts wrongly.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Everything else follows from that: the two update rules are the correction, \(\alpha\) sets its size, and the linear threshold is what limits it to linearly separable concepts.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** Write the Winnow-2 model and its decision rule.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: One weight per Boolean feature. The hypothesis is a linear threshold: predict 1 when \(\sum_i w_i x_i > \theta\), else 0.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** Walk through what Winnow-2 does with a single training example.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Score the example, threshold the score to a prediction, compare with the truth, and update only if they differ -- promoting on a false negative, demoting on a false positive.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** In Winnow-2, explain promotion: when does it fire, and what does it do to the weights?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: It fires when the prediction was 0 but the truth is 1 -- the score was too low. Multiply every weight whose feature is 1 by \(\alpha\), raising the score next time.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** In Winnow-2, explain demotion: when does it fire, and what does it do to the weights?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: It fires when the prediction was 1 but the truth is 0 -- the score was too high. Divide every weight whose feature is 1 by \(\alpha\), lowering the score next time.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** Why does Winnow-2 update only when it makes a mistake?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: A correct prediction is evidence the weights already work for that example, so moving them could only break what is right. Mistakes are the only signal that something needs to change.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** Why does an update leave the weights of inactive features untouched?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: A feature equal to 0 contributes nothing to the score, so it cannot have caused the mistake. Only features that were on share the blame.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** Winnow-2's updates are multiplicative rather than additive. What does that buy?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Weights can shrink toward zero or grow quickly across orders of magnitude, so an irrelevant feature is suppressed in a few updates rather than being slowly subtracted away.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** Explain why Winnow-2 can learn AND but cannot learn XOR.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Its hypothesis is a single linear threshold, so it can only express linearly separable concepts. AND is separable by a line; XOR has no linear separator at all.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** What role does the threshold play in Winnow-2, and what happens if it is set badly?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: It sets how large the score must be to predict 1. Badly chosen against \(\alpha\), promotions and demotions undo each other and the mistake count cycles instead of converging.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** Write pseudocode for training Winnow-2.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Predict with the threshold rule, and update multiplicatively only on a mistake.
:Q_STEPS: WINNOW-2-TRAIN(X, alpha, theta): | w[i] <- 1 for i = 1..d | repeat until no mistakes (or one epoch): | .  for each example (x, r) in X: | .  .  f <- sum over i of w[i] * x[i] | .  .  yhat <- 1 if f > theta else 0 | .  .  if yhat == r: continue | .  .  if r == 1 and yhat == 0:            // promotion | .  .  .  for each i with x[i] == 1: w[i] <- alpha * w[i] | .  .  if r == 0 and yhat == 1:            // demotion | .  .  .  for each i with x[i] == 1: w[i] <- w[i] / alpha | return w
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** Write pseudocode for classifying a point with a trained Winnow-2 model.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Score the active features and compare against the threshold.
:Q_STEPS: WINNOW-2-CLASSIFY(x, w, theta): | f <- sum over i of w[i] * x[i] | return 1 if f > theta else 0
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

:PROPERTIES:

:Q_UNIT: found

** On a promotion, Winnow-2 updates the weights of the active features by:
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: mc
:Q_OPTIONS: multiplying by alpha | dividing by alpha | adding alpha | subtracting alpha
:Q_ANSWER: multiplying by alpha
:Q_EXPLAIN: Promotion fires when the true label is 1 and the prediction was 0 -- the score was too low, so the active weights are scaled up. Demotion divides by alpha.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** Winnow-2 updates its weights after every training example.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: tf
:Q_ANSWER: False
:Q_EXPLAIN: It is mistake-driven: a correct prediction means the weights already order this example correctly, so changing them could only break what works.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** A Winnow-2 update leaves the weights of features with x_i = 0 unchanged.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: tf
:Q_ANSWER: True
:Q_EXPLAIN: An inactive feature contributed nothing to the score, so it bears no blame for the mistake. This is what lets Winnow-2 tolerate many irrelevant features.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** Winnow-2's decision rule predicts class 1 when the weighted sum is strictly greater than __________.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: fill
:Q_ANSWER: theta
:Q_ACCEPT: the threshold | theta (the threshold)
:Q_EXPLAIN: f = sum_i w_i x_i, predict 1 if f > theta. Theta sets where the linear boundary sits; set badly it can make one class unreachable.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** Winnow-2 cannot learn XOR because:
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: mc
:Q_OPTIONS: XOR is not linearly separable | XOR needs more than two features | its updates are multiplicative | its threshold cannot be tuned
:Q_ANSWER: XOR is not linearly separable
:Q_EXPLAIN: A threshold on a weighted sum is a hyperplane -- a representation bias, not a search failure. No weight setting expresses XOR, so no amount of data helps.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

* Why Learning Is Possible

** What is inductive bias?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: The assumptions a learner adds to the data so that its output follows. It is the minimal set of assertions that, with the training examples, entails the learner's prediction on new instances.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** Explain why a learner with no inductive bias cannot generalize, even with unlimited data.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: With no assumptions, every labeling of an unseen instance is equally consistent with the training data. The learner has no rational basis for preferring one, so it cannot predict.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** Explain representation bias.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: The restriction the model's form places on what can be expressed at all -- the shape of the hypothesis space. A linear threshold simply cannot represent XOR.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** Explain preference bias.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: How the learner searches the hypothesis space and ranks the hypotheses it can express. Choosing the highest-gain split, or the simplest consistent rule, is preference bias.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** Compare representation bias and preference bias, with an example of each.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Representation fixes what is expressible, preference fixes which expressible thing is chosen. Axis-parallel splits are representation; picking the highest-information-gain one is preference.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** Explain Occam's Razor as a learning principle, and what it protects against.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Prefer the simplest hypothesis consistent with the data. A simpler model has less capacity to fit noise, so it protects against overfitting.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** Explain the No Free Lunch theorem and what it implies about choosing an algorithm.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Averaged over every possible target function, all algorithms perform identically. So no algorithm is universally best -- you must match the algorithm to the problem.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** If No Free Lunch holds, what are you actually claiming when you call an algorithm "good"?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Only that its inductive bias happens to match this data. The claim is about the fit between assumption and problem, never about the algorithm alone.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** Explain the inductive learning hypothesis and the assumption it rests on.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: A hypothesis that fits a large enough training sample will also fit unseen examples. It assumes future data is drawn from the same distribution as the training data.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** Restricting which hypotheses can be expressed at all is a __________ bias.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: fill
:Q_ANSWER: representation
:Q_EXPLAIN: Preference bias instead orders hypotheses the learner could all express -- as a greedy tree-grower prefers shorter trees.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** Which of these is a preference bias rather than a representation bias?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: mc
:Q_OPTIONS: ID3 preferring shorter trees | Winnow-2 allowing only linear boundaries | a standard tree testing one feature per node | conjunctions of literals as the hypothesis space
:Q_ANSWER: ID3 preferring shorter trees
:Q_EXPLAIN: The other three limit what is expressible. ID3's greedy search could express a large tree but orders its search to find a small one first.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** A learner with no inductive bias can still generalize, given enough data.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: tf
:Q_ANSWER: False
:Q_EXPLAIN: With no bias, every labeling of the unseen instances remains in the version space, so the learner has no grounds to prefer one prediction over another. Bias is what makes generalization possible at all.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** The No Free Lunch theorem implies that:
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: mc
:Q_OPTIONS: no algorithm beats all others averaged over all possible problems | no algorithm can learn any problem | more data never helps | every algorithm has the same bias
:Q_ANSWER: no algorithm beats all others averaged over all possible problems
:Q_EXPLAIN: So calling an algorithm "good" is a claim about a match between its bias and the problems you actually face -- never a claim about all problems.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

* Measuring Error

** Explain true error, and why you can never measure it.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: The probability a hypothesis misclassifies an instance drawn from the underlying distribution. You only ever observe a finite sample from that distribution, so it can only be estimated.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** Explain sample error, and why it is the one you can actually compute.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: The proportion of a given data sample the hypothesis gets wrong. It is a count over data you hold, so it is directly computable -- also called empirical or training error.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** Why does the gap between sample error and true error matter?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Sample error can be driven to zero by memorizing, while true error stays high. The whole point of learning theory is bounding how far apart the two can be.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** A classmate reports 2% training error as evidence their model is good. What is wrong with the claim?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Training error measures capacity, not quality -- a complex enough model drives it to zero by memorizing. Ask for error on data never used in fitting or tuning.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** The error you can actually compute from your data is the __________ error.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: fill
:Q_ANSWER: sample
:Q_ACCEPT: sample error | empirical
:Q_EXPLAIN: True error is over the whole distribution, which you never have. Every bound in the course exists to relate the one you can measure to the one you care about.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** True error can be measured exactly given a large enough test set.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: tf
:Q_ANSWER: False
:Q_EXPLAIN: It is defined over the entire distribution. A larger test set tightens the estimate of it, but it stays an estimate.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

* Loss Functions

** Explain 0/1 loss, and give its main drawback as a training objective.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: It charges 1 for a wrong prediction and 0 for a right one. It is the natural error count, but it is flat almost everywhere, so gradient methods get no signal from it.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** Explain squared loss and what it is sensitive to.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: \(L^{2}(\hat{y},y) = (\hat{y}-y)^{2}\), the squared residual. Squaring makes it very sensitive to large errors, so a few outliers can dominate the fit.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** Explain hinge loss and when it stops charging anything.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: \(L_{hinge}(\hat{y},y) = \max\{0, 1 - \hat{y}y\}\). The loss falls to zero once \(\hat{y}y\) reaches 1, so being merely correct is not enough -- it must be correct by that much.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** Explain absolute error and how it compares with squared loss.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: It charges the magnitude of the residual rather than its square. Outliers pull it far less, making it the more robust choice on heavy-tailed data.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** Explain log loss, and what the model's output has to look like for it to work.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: \(-[y\log\hat{y} + (1-y)\log(1-\hat{y})]\), scoring a predicted probability. The output must lie strictly between 0 and 1 -- hence a sigmoid -- since the log diverges at the ends.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** Your data has a few extreme outliers. Compare squared loss and absolute error for it.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Squared loss squares those residuals, so the fit bends toward the outliers. Absolute error weights them linearly and largely ignores them. Prefer absolute error, or transform the target.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** You want to train a classifier whose output is squashed into (0,1) by a sigmoid, using gradient descent. Compare 0/1 loss and log loss for the job.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: 0/1 loss is flat almost everywhere, so gradient descent gets no signal from it, and it discards the output value entirely. Log loss scores that value directly and is differentiable.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** Explain the difference between a loss function and a risk function.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Loss scores a single prediction. Risk is the total over the whole data set, plus any regularization penalty. Training minimizes risk.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** Write the risk function, and explain what its second term is for.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: \(\ell(\theta) = \sum_i L(g_\theta(x^{(i)}), r^{(i)}) + \lambda\rho(\theta)\). The second term penalizes large or complex parameters, trading training fit for simplicity.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** What does the regularization weight control, and what happens at each extreme?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: How much simplicity is bought at the cost of fit. Large: the model is heavily constrained, high bias and low variance. Near zero: it fits freely, low bias and high variance.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** Which loss function is the natural fit for a model whose output is a probability?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: mc
:Q_OPTIONS: log loss (cross entropy) | 0/1 loss | squared loss | hinge loss
:Q_ANSWER: log loss (cross entropy)
:Q_EXPLAIN: 0/1 loss suits a hard class label, squared loss a continuous target, hinge loss a margin classifier. Log loss needs the output to be a probability to take its logarithm.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** The main drawback of 0/1 loss as a training objective is that it is:
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: mc
:Q_OPTIONS: flat almost everywhere, so it gives no gradient | unbounded | sensitive to outliers | only defined for two classes
:Q_ANSWER: flat almost everywhere, so it gives no gradient
:Q_EXPLAIN: It also scores a barely-wrong prediction the same as a wildly wrong one, so it cannot tell a nearly-correct model from a hopeless one.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** Hinge loss stops charging anything once the product of prediction and label reaches __________.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: fill
:Q_ANSWER: 1
:Q_ACCEPT: one
:Q_EXPLAIN: L_hinge = max{0, 1 - yhat*y}, so a correct prediction clear of the margin by 1 costs nothing -- the model stops being pushed once it is confidently right.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** Which loss is least disturbed by a few extreme outliers?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: mc
:Q_OPTIONS: absolute error | squared loss | log loss | hinge loss
:Q_ANSWER: absolute error
:Q_EXPLAIN: Squared loss grows quadratically in the residual, so one far-off point can dominate the whole sum and drag the fit toward it.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** In the risk function, lambda controls how much the regularization term counts against fitting the data.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: tf
:Q_ANSWER: True
:Q_EXPLAIN: At lambda = 0 the penalty vanishes and the model is free to overfit; very large lambda crushes the parameters and the model underfits.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** A risk function differs from a loss function in that it is computed over:
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: mc
:Q_OPTIONS: the whole training set, plus a regularization term | a single example | the test set only | the validation set only
:Q_ANSWER: the whole training set, plus a regularization term
:Q_EXPLAIN: Loss scores one prediction; risk sums loss over the data and adds lambda*rho(theta). Risk is the thing actually minimized during training.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

* Bias and Variance

** Explain bias as a source of error.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Error from assumptions too simple for the truth -- the average model is systematically off. High bias means underfitting: real structure is missed.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** Explain variance as a source of error.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Error from sensitivity to the particular training sample -- redraw the data and the model changes a lot. High variance means overfitting: noise is being learned.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** Explain irreducible error, and why no model can remove it.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Noise in the response itself, independent of any model. The same input can produce different outputs, so no function of the input can predict it.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** Write the decomposition of total error into its three parts.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Total Error = Bias\(^{2}\) + Variance + Irreducible Error. Only the first two are under your control.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** Explain the bias-variance trade-off as model complexity increases.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Bias falls as the model can represent more, but variance rises as it starts fitting the sample's noise. Total error dips then climbs, so the best model is intermediate.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** Your model has near-zero training error and poor test error. Diagnose it, and give two fixes.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: High variance -- it is overfitting. Fix by reducing capacity, raising regularization, adding training data, or pruning or smoothing the model.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** Your model has high error on both training and test data. Diagnose it, and give two fixes.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: High bias -- it is underfitting, and more data will not help. Fix by increasing model capacity, adding features, or lowering regularization.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** Total error decomposes into bias squared, variance, and:
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: mc
:Q_OPTIONS: irreducible error | regularization | training error | the loss
:Q_ANSWER: irreducible error
:Q_EXPLAIN: Noise in the data itself. No model removes it, so it sets a floor that a reported error below should make you suspect leakage.
:Q_UNIT: found
:END:

** Near-zero training error with poor test error is a symptom of high variance.

* Linear Regression

** Explain what univariate linear regression fits and what it minimizes.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: A straight line \(h_w(x) = w_1x + w_0\) through the data, minimizing the sum of squared vertical residuals \(\sum_j (y_j - h_w(x_j))^{2}\).
:Q_UNIT: linreg
:END:

** Explain how the closed-form solution for linear regression is obtained.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: The squared-error surface is convex, so its minimum is where both partial derivatives vanish. Setting them to zero gives two linear equations with one solution.
:Q_UNIT: linreg
:END:

** Write the closed-form solution for the slope and the intercept of a fitted line.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: \(w_1 = \frac{N\sum x_jy_j - (\sum x_j)(\sum y_j)}{N\sum x_j^{2} - (\sum x_j)^{2}}\) and \(w_0 = \frac{\sum y_j - w_1\sum x_j}{N}\). Solve for the slope first.
:Q_UNIT: linreg
:END:

** Explain what weight space is and why it is a useful way to think about learning.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: The space of all possible weight settings, with the loss as a surface over it. It turns learning into navigating that surface toward a minimum.
:Q_UNIT: linreg
:END:

** Explain why maximum likelihood estimation under Gaussian noise gives least squares.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: The Gaussian likelihood is an exponential of the negative squared residual. Taking logs turns the product into a sum of squared residuals, and the constants drop out of the argmax.
:Q_UNIT: linreg
:END:

** What assumption about the noise is being made when you fit by least squares?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: That the residuals are Gaussian with constant variance, independent across examples. Heavy tails or changing variance break the justification.
:Q_UNIT: linreg
:END:

** Write pseudocode for fitting a line in closed form.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Accumulate four sums in one pass, then solve for slope and intercept.
:Q_STEPS: FIT-LINE(points (x[j], y[j]) for j = 1..N): | Sx <- 0; Sy <- 0; Sxy <- 0; Sxx <- 0 | for j = 1..N: | .  Sx  <- Sx  + x[j] | .  Sy  <- Sy  + y[j] | .  Sxy <- Sxy + x[j] * y[j] | .  Sxx <- Sxx + x[j] * x[j] | denom <- N * Sxx - Sx * Sx | if denom == 0: fail      // every x identical | w1 <- (N * Sxy - Sx * Sy) / denom | w0 <- (Sy - w1 * Sx) / N | return (w0, w1)
:Q_UNIT: linreg
:END:

** Write pseudocode for fitting weights by gradient descent.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Repeatedly step the weights against the averaged gradient of the squared-error loss.
:Q_STEPS: GRADIENT-DESCENT(X, y, eta): | w <- small random vector | repeat until converged: | .  grad <- zero vector | .  for i = 1..N: | .  .  resid <- f(x[i], w) - y[i] | .  .  grad  <- grad + resid * x[i] | .  grad <- grad / N | .  w <- w - eta * grad | return w
:Q_UNIT: linreg
:END:

** Compare the closed-form solution with gradient descent. When would you need the iterative method?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Closed form is exact and needs no tuning, but requires a solvable system. Use gradient descent for very many features, nonlinear models, or data arriving online.
:Q_UNIT: linreg
:END:

** Fitting by least squares is maximum likelihood estimation under the assumption that the noise is:
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: mc
:Q_OPTIONS: Gaussian with constant variance | uniform | Laplacian | absent
:Q_ANSWER: Gaussian with constant variance
:Q_EXPLAIN: Maximizing the Gaussian likelihood turns the exponent into a sum of squared residuals, so minimizing squared error and maximizing likelihood are the same operation.
:Q_UNIT: linreg
:END:

** Univariate linear regression minimizes the sum of __________ residuals.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: fill
:Q_ANSWER: squared
:Q_ACCEPT: squared (SSE) | square
:Q_EXPLAIN: Which is what makes the closed form available: the derivative of a quadratic is linear, so setting it to zero gives a solvable system.
:Q_UNIT: linreg
:END:

** Gradient descent is needed instead of the closed form when:
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: mc
:Q_OPTIONS: the matrix inverse is too expensive or does not exist | the data is noisy | the features are categorical | the target is continuous
:Q_ANSWER: the matrix inverse is too expensive or does not exist
:Q_EXPLAIN: With many features the inversion dominates, and with collinear features the system is singular. Gradient descent needs neither.
:Q_UNIT: linreg
:END:

* Version Spaces

** What does it mean for a hypothesis to be consistent with the training data?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: It agrees with the true label on every training example seen so far. Consistency is all-or-nothing -- one disagreement disqualifies it.
:Q_UNIT: theory
:END:

** Explain what a version space is.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: The set of every hypothesis in the space that is still consistent with the data. It shrinks as examples arrive, ideally to the target concept.
:Q_UNIT: theory
:END:

** Why track only the most specific and most general boundaries rather than the whole version space?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Every consistent hypothesis lies between them under the generality ordering, so the two boundary sets represent the whole space compactly -- often far smaller than enumerating it.
:Q_UNIT: theory
:END:

** Explain what a positive example does to the two boundaries, and why.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: It generalizes the specific boundary, which must now cover it, and prunes anything in the general boundary that fails to. Positives can only loosen the specific side.
:Q_UNIT: theory
:END:

** Explain what a negative example does to the two boundaries, and why.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: It specializes the general boundary, which must now exclude it, and prunes anything in the specific boundary that covers it. Negatives can only tighten the general side.
:Q_UNIT: theory
:END:

** Why does a single mislabeled example break candidate elimination?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: The true concept is inconsistent with the bad label, so it gets eliminated permanently. The algorithm has no way to reinstate it, and the space can collapse to empty.
:Q_UNIT: theory
:END:

** Why is candidate elimination said to have no preference bias?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: It keeps every consistent hypothesis instead of choosing among them, so it expresses no preference. All of its bias is in the hypothesis language.
:Q_UNIT: theory
:END:

** How can a partially converged version space classify a new instance, and when can it not?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Poll its hypotheses: unanimous agreement gives a confident label. A split vote leaves the instance undetermined -- and that is exactly the instance worth querying.
:Q_UNIT: theory
:END:

** Write pseudocode for candidate elimination.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Start the boundaries as far apart as the language allows, then generalize on positives and specialize on negatives.
:Q_STEPS: CANDIDATE-ELIMINATION(D): | G <- the maximally general hypotheses | S <- the maximally specific hypotheses | for each example e in D: | .  if e is POSITIVE: | .  .  drop from G anything inconsistent with e | .  .  replace each inconsistent s in S by its minimal generalizations | .  .  keep only those some g in G is more general than | .  .  prune from S anything more general than another member | .  else: | .  .  drop from S anything inconsistent with e | .  .  replace each inconsistent g in G by its minimal specializations | .  .  keep only those some s in S is more specific than | .  .  prune from G anything more specific than another member | return (S, G)
:Q_UNIT: theory
:END:

** A positive example causes candidate elimination to:
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: mc
:Q_OPTIONS: generalize S and prune G | specialize G and prune S | generalize both | discard the example
:Q_ANSWER: generalize S and prune G
:Q_EXPLAIN: S must stretch minimally to admit the example; anything in G inconsistent with it is dropped. A negative example does the mirror image.
:Q_UNIT: theory
:END:

** Candidate elimination has a representation bias but no preference bias.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: tf
:Q_ANSWER: True
:Q_EXPLAIN: It keeps every consistent hypothesis rather than ordering them, so it expresses no preference. Its bias is entirely in the hypothesis language it can represent.
:Q_UNIT: theory
:END:

** A single mislabeled example breaks candidate elimination because:
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: mc
:Q_OPTIONS: it can eliminate the true concept, and nothing restores it | it slows the search | it widens the version space | it makes S and G equal
:Q_ANSWER: it can eliminate the true concept, and nothing restores it
:Q_EXPLAIN: Elimination is monotone and permanent. The algorithm has no notion of an example possibly being wrong, so the version space can collapse to empty.
:Q_UNIT: theory
:END:

* How Many Examples Are Enough

** Explain what sample complexity measures.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: How many training examples a learner needs to reach a target accuracy with a target confidence. It depends on the hypothesis space and on how examples are supplied.
:Q_UNIT: theory
:END:

** Compare a sufficient and a necessary bound on sample complexity.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: A sufficient (upper) bound guarantees success with that many examples. A necessary (lower) bound says no learner can succeed with fewer. The truth lies between them.
:Q_UNIT: theory
:END:

** Explain the optimal query strategy for an active learner, and the number of queries it needs.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Always ask about an instance that splits the remaining hypotheses in half -- twenty questions. That needs about \(\lg|\mathcal{H}|\) queries.
:Q_UNIT: theory
:END:

** What does it mean for a version space to be epsilon-exhausted, and why is that the useful property?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Every surviving hypothesis has true error below \(\epsilon\). It is useful because you can then return ANY consistent hypothesis without needing to pick cleverly.
:Q_UNIT: theory
:END:

** State the Haussler bound and explain what it is bounding.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: For finite \(\mathcal{H}\) and \(m\) independent examples, the probability the version space is NOT \(\epsilon\)-exhausted is at most \(|\mathcal{H}|e^{-\epsilon m}\) -- the chance a bad hypothesis survives.
:Q_UNIT: theory
:END:

** Derive the required number of examples from the Haussler bound.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Demand \(|\mathcal{H}|e^{-\epsilon m} \leq \delta\), take logs, and solve: \(m \geq \frac{1}{\epsilon}(\ln|\mathcal{H}| + \ln\frac{1}{\delta})\).
:Q_UNIT: theory
:END:

** For conjunctions over n Boolean attributes, explain why the hypothesis space has size 3 to the n.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Each attribute appears in one of three ways: required true, required false, or don't care. Those choices are independent across the \(n\) attributes.
:Q_UNIT: theory
:END:

** Explain agnostic learning and what assumption it gives up.

:Q_UNIT: theory

** Explain why the agnostic bound needs far more examples than the consistent one.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: With consistency, each example is a chance to eliminate a bad hypothesis outright. Agnostically you must ESTIMATE every error rate accurately, and concentration costs \(1/\epsilon^{2}\) rather than \(1/\epsilon\).
:Q_UNIT: theory

** Explain why a completely unbiased learner needs exponentially many examples.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Allowing every possible labeling makes \(|\mathcal{H}| = 2^{2^{n}}\), so \(\ln|\mathcal{H}|\) grows as \(2^{n}\). The quantitative form of "an unbiased learner cannot learn".
:Q_UNIT: theory
:END:

** Explain what PAC learnability requires.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: For every concept and every input distribution, the learner must output a hypothesis with error at most \(\epsilon\), with probability at least \(1-\delta\).
:Q_UNIT: theory
:END:

** In "probably approximately correct", what does each half refer to?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: "Probably" is the confidence \(1-\delta\) -- we may fail outright, rarely. "Approximately correct" is the accuracy \(\epsilon\) -- even on success the hypothesis is not perfect.
:Q_UNIT: theory
:END:

** What does efficient PAC-learnability add to plain PAC-learnability?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: A runtime requirement: polynomial in \(1/\epsilon\), \(1/\delta\), the instance length, and the size of the concept. Polynomial sample complexity alone is not enough.
:Q_UNIT: theory
:END:

** For conjunctions over n Boolean attributes, the size of the hypothesis space is:
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: mc
:Q_OPTIONS: 3 to the n | 2 to the n | n squared | 2 to the 2 to the n
:Q_ANSWER: 3 to the n
:Q_EXPLAIN: Each attribute appears positively, negatively, or not at all -- three choices, independently, for each of n attributes.
:Q_UNIT: theory
:END:

** The Haussler bound bounds the probability that:
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: mc
:Q_OPTIONS: some consistent hypothesis still has true error above epsilon | the sample error exceeds epsilon | the learner runs too long | the version space is empty
:Q_ANSWER: some consistent hypothesis still has true error above epsilon
:Q_EXPLAIN: That is the version space failing to be epsilon-exhausted. Bounding it at delta and solving for m gives the sample complexity.
:Q_UNIT: theory
:END:

** In the sample complexity bound, the dependence on 1/delta is logarithmic while the dependence on 1/epsilon is linear.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: tf
:Q_ANSWER: True
:Q_EXPLAIN: m >= (1/epsilon)(ln|H| + ln(1/delta)): demanding higher confidence is cheap, demanding higher accuracy is not.

** Agnostic learning gives up the assumption that:
:PROPERTIES:

:Q_ANSWER: the target concept lies in the hypothesis space
:Q_EXPLAIN: With no consistent hypothesis to find, the learner returns the best available one -- and the bound needs an epsilon-squared in the denominator rather than epsilon, so far more examples.
:Q_UNIT: theory
:END:

* VC Dimension

** Explain what it means for a hypothesis space to shatter a set of points.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: For every one of the \(2^{N}\) ways of labeling those \(N\) points, some hypothesis in the space realizes it. The space can express any labeling of that set.
:Q_UNIT: theory
:END:

** Explain what VC dimension measures.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: The size of the largest point set the hypothesis space can shatter -- a measure of expressiveness that works for infinite spaces.
:Q_UNIT: theory
:END:

** Why is VC dimension needed at all when the size of the hypothesis space already exists?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Many useful spaces are infinite, so counting hypotheses cannot distinguish them. Rectangles and arbitrary convex polygons are both infinite but hugely different in power.
:Q_UNIT: theory
:END:

** To establish a VC dimension you must do two different things. What are they?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Exhibit ONE set of size \(N\) that is shattered, and show NO set of size \(N+1\) is. The lower half needs an example, the upper half an argument about all sets.
:Q_UNIT: theory
:END:

** Show that intervals on the real line have VC dimension 2.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Two points admit all four labelings using intervals. For three points in order, \((+,-,+)\) is impossible: any interval covering the outer two must cover the middle one.
:Q_UNIT: theory
:END:

** Explain why a line in the plane has VC dimension 3.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Three non-collinear points can be split every one of 8 ways, since any one can be cut off from the others. No set of four can be, so the dimension is 3.
:Q_UNIT: theory
:END:

** What is the VC dimension of a linear decision surface in d dimensions?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: \(d+1\). It matches the plane case, where a line in two dimensions shatters three points.
:Q_UNIT: theory
:END:

** Explain the relationship between VC dimension and the size of a finite hypothesis space.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: \(VC(\mathcal{H}) \leq \lg|\mathcal{H}|\). Shattering \(m\) points needs a distinct hypothesis per labeling, so \(|\mathcal{H}|\) must exceed \(2^{m}\).
:Q_UNIT: theory
:END:

** Explain how VC dimension relates to the strength of a learner's inductive bias.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: A high VC dimension means more labelings are expressible, so fewer are ruled out a priori -- weaker bias. A completely unbiased space shatters everything.
:Q_UNIT: theory
:END:

* Making Decisions Under Cost

** Explain risk minimization and why a cost matrix is needed at all.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Choose the action minimizing expected loss, \(R(\alpha_i \mid x) = \sum_j \lambda_{ij}P(\omega_j \mid x)\). The matrix is needed because different mistakes rarely cost the same.
:Q_UNIT: theory
:END:

** Explain what happens to the decision rule when the cost matrix is 0/1.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: All mistakes cost the same, so minimizing risk collapses to maximizing the posterior -- just pick the most probable class.
:Q_UNIT: theory
:END:

** A missed cancer diagnosis costs far more than a false alarm. Explain how that changes the decision boundary.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: It shifts toward predicting positive: a smaller posterior probability now justifies it. You accept more false alarms to avoid the expensive misses.
:Q_UNIT: theory
:END:

** Explain the maximum a posteriori hypothesis and which factor drops out of it.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: \(h_{MAP} = \arg\max_h P(\mathcal{D} \mid h)P(h)\) -- likelihood times prior. The evidence \(P(\mathcal{D})\) drops because it does not depend on \(h\).
:Q_UNIT: theory
:END:

** Compare the MAP hypothesis with the maximum likelihood hypothesis.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: MAP weights the likelihood by a prior over hypotheses; ML ignores the prior. They coincide exactly when all priors are equal.
:Q_UNIT: theory
:END:

** When the cost matrix is 0/1, risk minimization reduces to choosing:
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: mc
:Q_OPTIONS: the most probable class | the cheapest class | the majority class | the class with the largest likelihood
:Q_ANSWER: the most probable class
:Q_EXPLAIN: With every error costing the same, expected cost is just the probability of being wrong, so minimizing it means taking the maximum posterior -- h_MAP.
:Q_UNIT: theory
:END:

** The maximum likelihood hypothesis is the MAP hypothesis with the __________ dropped.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: fill
:Q_ANSWER: prior
:Q_ACCEPT: prior probability | P(h)
:Q_EXPLAIN: Equivalent to assuming a uniform prior -- defensible when you have no reason to favour any hypothesis, and quietly wrong when you do.
:Q_UNIT: theory
:END:

** Raising the cost of a false negative moves the decision boundary so that more examples are classified positive.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: tf
:Q_ANSWER: True
:Q_EXPLAIN: A missed cancer diagnosis costing far more than a false alarm means the threshold on the posterior drops -- you accept more false alarms to buy back misses.
:Q_UNIT: theory
:END:
