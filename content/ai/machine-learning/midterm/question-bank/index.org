* How the questions are built

Most are *free response*: you recall the answer, then *Reveal answer* shows the
model answer and you mark yourself. That layer carries the mechanism and the
pseudocode -- the parts the exam's open-ended section asks for.

On top of it sits an *objective layer* of multiple-choice, true/false and
fill-in questions, matching the objective section of the exam. These pin the
facts the free-response answers leave soft: the four metric axioms, the
VC-dimension table, the three linkage types, which loss suits which output, the
Minkowski special cases, the bootstrap percentages, the VC dimension of a
=d=-dimensional linear surface. They are deliberately not restatements -- where a
free-response question asks you to explain single-, complete- and average-link,
the objective version asks which one is the largest pairwise distance.

Every type is still *self-assessed*: multiple-choice and true/false render their
options and fill-in renders a text box, but nothing is graded. *Reveal answer*
shows the right answer and its =:Q_EXPLAIN:= note, and you mark your own. One
consequence for *Flashcards* mode: it shows a question's prompt without its
options, so every objective stem is written to stand on its own as a card.

*Pseudocode questions* reveal a monospace outline under the model answer. In
those outlines, *leading dots mark indentation depth*, since the single-line Org
property that carries them cannot hold real indentation. They cover the
algorithms worth being able to write from memory: Winnow-2, k-NN classification
and regression, edited and condensed NN, KD-tree build and query, LOF, hill
climbing, simulated annealing, genetic algorithms, PCA, Fisher's LDA, k-means,
k-means++, EM, fuzzy c-means, agglomerative clustering, silhouette, candidate
elimination, ID3, regression trees, numeric split selection, reduced error
pruning, resolution, sequential covering, K-fold and 5x2 cross-validation, and
both project pipelines end to end.

*Calculation questions* are generated fresh on every =New test=, so the numbers
change but the method does not. They cover entropy and information gain, gain
ratio, intrinsic value, a plain expected value, precision/recall/F-beta, micro
versus macro averaging, the Haussler and PAC and VC sample-complexity bounds,
the silhouette coefficient, pseudo-F, LOF, Minkowski distance, VDM, a Winnow-2
weight update, a Gaussian KDE, the k-NN posterior, the bootstrap percentages,
and a regression-tree leaf MSE. Each one reveals its worked steps so you can
check your arithmetic against the method.

* Adding to the bank

Add a level-2 heading on the relevant page with a =:Q_TYPE:= drawer and it shows
up on every test that includes its =:Q_UNIT:=, with no other edits needed:

#+begin_example
,** Write the gain ratio and explain what it guards against.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: gainRatio(f) = gain(f) / IV(f) ...
:Q_STEPS: STEP-ONE(...): | .  pipe-separated lines render as a monospace block
:Q_UNIT: trees
:END:
#+end_example

An objective question is the same drawer with a different =:Q_TYPE:=. Use =mc=
with pipe-separated =:Q_OPTIONS:= (no pipes inside an option), =tf= with
=:Q_ANSWER:= exactly =True= or =False=, or =fill= with =:Q_ACCEPT:= listing the
variants you would also accept. =:Q_EXPLAIN:= is what the reveal shows under the
answer, so it is the place to put the contrast that makes the fact stick:

#+begin_example
,** Which linkage merges on the largest distance between any pair of points across the two clusters?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: mc
:Q_OPTIONS: complete-link | single-link | average-link | divisive
:Q_ANSWER: complete-link
:Q_EXPLAIN: Single-link takes the smallest such distance; average-link uses the cluster averages.
:Q_UNIT: clust
:END:
#+end_example

Keep multiple-choice distractors inside the course material -- a sibling from the
same slide, never an invented term, since a plausible fabricated option is still
something to misremember.
