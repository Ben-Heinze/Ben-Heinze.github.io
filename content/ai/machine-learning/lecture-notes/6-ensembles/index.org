* Rule Pruning

- Two approaches to pruning:
  - Prune whole rules
  - Prune predicates within rules
- Pruning predicates subsumes
  pruning rules --- dropping every
  predicate from a rule is the same as dropping the rule, so you don't need to do
  both. Given a rule $p \wedge q \wedge r \to y$, pruning predicates can reach any
  of its generalizations, including the empty one.
- Sort the rules by coverage.
- The resulting pruning strategy, similar to decision trees, is
  Incremental Reduced Error Pruning (IREP).

The data split is the same idea as reduced-error pruning for trees:
grow using 2/3 of the data, prune using 1/3 of the data.
Growing on
one subset and evaluating removals on a held-out subset is what keeps pruning from
simply undoing the fit.

** The =PruneRule= function

Let $\mathit{Prune\,Pos}$ and $\mathit{Prune\,Neg}$ be the positive and negative
halves of the pruning set. For the current rule, let $p$ be the number of examples
in $\mathit{Prune\,Pos}$ covered by the rule, and $n$ the number of examples in
$\mathit{Prune\,Neg}$ covered by it. Define

\[
  v(\textit{Rule}, \mathit{Prune\,Pos}, \mathit{Prune\,Neg})
    = \frac{p - n}{p + n}
\]

Choose the predicate to prune that maximizes $v$. The numerator rewards a rule for
covering positives rather than negatives; the denominator normalizes by how much the
rule covers at all, so a rule that covers two positives and no negatives doesn't
automatically beat one covering twenty positives and one negative on raw counts
alone.

** RIPPER

RIPPER --- "Repeated Incremental Pruning to Produce Error Reduction".
Given a rule set $\textit{RuleSet} = \{\textit{Rule}_1, \ldots, \textit{Rule}_k\}$,
there are two options at each step:

1. *Replace Rule* --- judged on total error.
2. *Revise Rule* --- modify the rule's predicates.

Decisions are made on Minimum Description Length.

*** Minimum Description Length

An information-theoretic approach to model selection. Rather than choosing the
hypothesis that fits best, choose the one for which /model plus data/ is cheapest
to describe. Starting from the MAP hypothesis,

\[
  h_{\mathit{MAP}} = \arg\max_{h \in H} P(D \mid h)\, P(h)
                   = \arg\max_{h \in H} \big[ \log_2 P(D \mid h) + \log_2 P(h) \big]
                   = \arg\min_{h \in H} \big[ -\log_2 P(D \mid h) - \log_2 P(h) \big]
\]

By Shannon's source coding result, the optimal code length for an event of
probability $p$ is $-\log_2 p$ bits. So writing

\[
  L(D \mid h) = -\log_2 P(D \mid h)
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  L(h) = -\log_2 P(h)
\]

the expression above becomes

\[
  h_{\mathit{MDL}} = \arg\min_{h \in H} \big[ L(D \mid h) + L(h) \big]
\]

$L(h)$ is the cost of describing the rule set and $L(D \mid h)$ the cost of
describing the data's exceptions to it. MDL is therefore the same
preference for simplicity that pruning expresses, stated in bits
--- and that
is why RIPPER can use one criterion to decide between replacing and revising a rule.

* Ensembles

** Motivation

- It is difficult to determine the appropriate inductive bias for a learning method
  with respect to a target data set.
- Effect: susceptibility to either overfitting (high bias / low variance) or
  underfitting (low bias / high variance).
- Approach:
  - Train multiple base learners (models)
  - Combine predictions from the base learners
- Intended result: simultaneous reduction of bias and variance.
  - *CAUTION!*
  - The bias--variance tradeoff still applies.
  - The "No Free Lunch" theorems still apply.

That caution is the honest framing: an ensemble does not escape the tradeoff, it
sidesteps the problem of having to /pick/ one inductive bias up front.

** Problem statement

Given:

- A training data set $D$ for supervised learning, drawn from a common instance
  space $X$.
- A collection of inductive learning algorithms.

Hypotheses are produced by applying inducers to subsets $D_i \subseteq D$, where the
subsets are obtained by sampling, transformation, partitioning, and so on. Consider
those hypotheses as $|H|$ definitions of prediction algorithms --- the
base learners.

Return: a new prediction algorithm --- not necessarily in $H$ --- for $x \in X$ that
combines the outputs of the collection of prediction algorithms.

** The three families

| Bagging  | Short for "bootstrap aggregation". Generate multiple sets of bootstrap samples, train on each set, apply basic aggregation. |
| Boosting | A sequential process. Each learner is trained based on the performance of previous learners; apply a weighted aggregation scheme. |
| Stacking | Train /different/ base learners (i.e. different algorithms), then train an aggregator to determine how best to combine them. |

** Critical factors

Two things an ensemble needs, and they pull against each other:

- *Accuracy*
  - The expectation is that the base learners be trained to be as accurate as
    possible.
  - But some ensemble methods only require weak learners
    --- learners
    capable of performing only slightly better than chance.
  - In those methods accuracy comes from the aggregation process, not from any
    individual learner.
- *Diversity*
  - The base learners need to have knowledge about different aspects of the task.
  - Achievable in several ways: diverse training sets, reweighting of the same
    training set, or applying multiple methods with different inductive biases ---
    which maps onto bagging, boosting and stacking respectively.

** General approaches

*Parallel* --- train base learners independently, combine the results through simple
aggregation. Input data goes to Learner 1, Learner 2, ..., Learner $n$ at once, and
their outputs are aggregated into a prediction.

*Sequential* --- train base learners iteratively, where the focus of training is
correcting errors from previous learners. Input data flows through Learner 1 to
Learner 2 to Learner $n$, and the prediction comes off the end of the chain.

Bagging and random forests are parallel; boosting is sequential.

** Measuring diversity

The goal is to train a set of base learners negatively correlated with one another. Ensemble diversity can be computed pairwise or non-pairwise. Let $H$
denote the ensemble of base learners.

*Pairwise --- Yule's Q statistic.* Let $I_{j,i}$ indicate whether learner $i$
recognizes data item $j$ correctly, and let $N^{ab}$ denote the number of times
$I_{j,i} = a$ and $I_{j,k} = b$. Then for the pair of learners $i$ and $k$

\[
  Q_{i,k} = \frac{N^{11} N^{00} - N^{01} N^{10}}{N^{11} N^{00} + N^{01} N^{10}}
\]

and the ensemble measure averages over all pairs:

\[
  Q = \frac{2}{m(m-1)} \sum_{i} \sum_{k > i} Q_{i,k}
\]

Want Q as close to --1 as possible
--- $Q$ is positive when two
learners tend to succeed and fail on the same items, so negative values are the
disagreement we are after.

*Non-pairwise --- entropy.* Let $\rho(x_j)$ denote the number of learners that
correctly classify $x_j$. Then

\[
  E(H) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{j=1}^{n} \frac{1}{m - \lceil m/2 \rceil}
         \min\big(\rho(x_j),\; m - \rho(x_j)\big)
\]

Want E as close to +1 as possible.
The $\min$ is maximal when the
learners split evenly on an item, so the measure peaks when the ensemble disagrees
the most it can.

** Combining learners

The predictions of the base learners need to be combined into a final prediction.
Such combiners are often called aggregators. How you aggregate varies
by task (classification vs regression), and the type of ensemble generally dictates
the method:

- Bagging/boosting: apply a *static* combination rule after training.
- Stacking: *train* an aggregation model based on the performance of base learners.

*** Majority vote

Arguably the simplest method, and specific to classification. Given a set of base
learners $H$, let $d_{t,c} \in \{0, 1\}$ denote whether classifier $t$ indicated
class $c$. Then

\[
  \mathit{class} = \arg\max_{c \in C} \sum_{t=1}^{|H|} d_{t,c}
\]

*** Weighted majority vote

Adjust the majority vote by the performance of the base learners. With $d_{t,c}$ as
above, let

\[
  w_t = \frac{p_t}{\sum_{t=1}^{|H|} p_t}
\]

where $p_t$ is the estimated accuracy of learner $t$. Then

\[
  \mathit{class} = \arg\max_{c \in C} \sum_{t=1}^{|H|} w_t\, d_{t,c}
\]

The weights are normalized accuracies, so they sum to 1 and a learner that is barely
better than chance contributes proportionally little.

[The worked example of weighted majority vote (p20) is a slide image --- see the PDF.]

*** Simple aggregation for regression

The process is similar to classification: replace the vote with the (weighted) mean,
or the median. Let $\hat{y}_t$ denote the prediction from the \(t\)-th base learner,
and let

\[
  w_t = \frac{(1 / e_t)}{\sum_{t=1}^{|H|} (1 / e_t)}
\]

where $e_t$ is the estimated /error/ of learner $t$ --- note this inverts, where the
classification weights used accuracy directly. Then

\[
  \mathit{resp} = \frac{1}{|H|} \sum_{t=1}^{|H|} \hat{y}_t
  \qquad\text{(unweighted)}
  \qquad\qquad
  \mathit{resp} = \sum_{t=1}^{|H|} w_t\, \hat{y}_t
  \qquad\text{(weighted)}
\]

** Ensemble selection

Conventional wisdom says that as the number of base learners increases, accuracy and
resistance to overfitting should improve. Two things complicate that:

- As the number of base learners increases, so does computational complexity.
- As the number of base learners increases, diversity can start to decrease, thus /reducing/ the accuracy of the ensemble.

So there might be an "optimal" subset of base learners for a given ensemble ---
which makes this a subset selection
problem, the same problem as
feature selection (see the dimensionality reduction module in
[[file:../3-density-estimation/index.org][3 Density Estimation]]). Common approaches:

- Stepwise selection: forward, backward, local search
- Wrapper-based
- Filter-based

*** Example: forward model selection

A wrapper-based approach:

1. Begin with an empty ensemble.
2. Select the base learner from a library of base learners, either
   a. based on individual model performance on a validation set, or
   b. based on maximizing ensemble performance on a validation set.
3. Repeat step 2 as long as ensemble performance continues to improve, or until all
   models in the library have been examined.
4. Return the selected subset of base learners.

This assumes the models can be ordered on performance, but still requires evaluating
the ensemble as it is generated --- and that evaluation inside the loop is exactly
what makes it a wrapper
method rather than a filter.

** Bootstrap aggregation (bagging)

Perhaps the most basic method for constructing ensembles. It has strong statistical
properties, from which uncertainty quantification can be performed. It results in a
set of diverse base learners by virtue of their being trained on different subsets
of the training data.

The process:

1. Generate $b$ different bootstrap training sets $D_1, \ldots, D_b$, each of size
   $|D|$.
2. Each data set $D_i$ is generated by sampling from $D$
   with replacement
   until $|D|$ examples are selected.
3. Train $b$ different base learners, learner $H_i$ on data set $D_i$.
4. The combiner is (weighted) majority, or (weighted) mean/median.

REMARK: bagging is optimized when the base learners exhibit high variance and low bias, such as a decision tree.
That is the key pairing to
remember --- bagging averages variance away but cannot fix bias, so it wants
unstable, low-bias learners. Boosting wants the opposite.

** Random Forests

Breiman's definition:

#+begin_quote
A /random forest/ is a classifier consisting of a collection of tree-structured
classifiers $\{h(x, \Theta_k),\ k = 1, \ldots, m\}$ where the $\Theta_k$ are
independent, identically distributed random vectors and each tree casts a unit vote
for the most popular class at input $x$.
#+end_quote

- This can be adapted for regression using averaging instead of voting.
- A common variant also selects a random subset of /features/ from which to generate
  the trees --- so randomness enters twice, through the bootstrap sample and through
  the features available at each split.
- REMARK: several cases have been demonstrated where random forests show competitive performance on tasks often "solved" via deep learning.

[The random forest diagram (p28) is a slide image --- see the PDF.]

*** Properties

- The trees in a random forest are simpler and therefore easier to construct.
- RF ensembles have been shown to be highly accurate and resistant to overfitting
  --- ensembles naturally regularize.
- Individual trees are relatively easy to interpret, although full ensembles are
  not.
- RF ensembles provide a level of interpretability through the ability to do
  variable importance analysis.
- Technically, RF ensembles do not need cross-validation --- out-of-bag samples
  can be used for generalization assessment, since each tree has roughly
  a third of the data withheld from it by the bootstrap. Often we still
  cross-validate anyway.
- RF ensembles can also be used for /clustering/, by counting the number of times
  pairs of points appear in the same leaves across the set of trees. That count is a
  similarity measure learned by a supervised method --- a neat bridge to the
  clustering section below.
- There exist many variants.

*** Possible variants

- Individual trees have all features available, and grow with optimal splits fully
  on bootstrapped samples.
- Individual trees have a random subset of features available, and grow with optimal
  splits on bootstrapped samples.
- Individual trees are grown with random splits.
- Only "stumps" are used as base learners, each stump generated based on information
  gain.
- Only "random stumps" are used as base learners.
- Oblique (multivariate) splits are allowed when generating the trees.
- A "spatial" variant considers spatial correlation in selecting features.

** Boosting

Invented independently by Freund (1990) and Schapire (1990). A sequential method of
constructing ensembles by re-weighting the training data based on prior performance.

- As a sequential process, it stops generating base learners either when a tunable
  target number have been generated, or when the performance of the ensemble has
  plateaued.
- Assumes weak learners, consisting of high bias and low variance
  ---
  the mirror image of bagging's requirement.
- Tends to work poorly with noisy data, since it tends to over-weight
  the noisy examples. A mislabelled point is permanently misclassified, so
  reweighting keeps escalating its importance.

*** Basic boosting

The original boosting algorithm only built an ensemble of three base learners. For
training set $D$ with $|D| = n$:

1. Sample $n_1 < n$ examples $D_1 \subset D$ without replacement.
2. Train base learner $\rho_1$ on this sample.
3. Sample a second set of $n_2 < n$ examples $D_2 \subset D$ such that
   a. the first $n_2 / 2$ examples correspond to the examples from $D$ /misclassified/
      by $\rho_1$, and
   b. the remaining $n_2 / 2$ are drawn from $D$ without replacement but are
      /correctly/ classified by $\rho_1$.
4. Train base learner $\rho_2$ from $D_2$.
5. Apply $\rho_1$ and $\rho_2$ to $D$ and extract all examples where they
   disagree
   to yield $D_3 \subset D$.
6. Train $\rho_3$ on $D_3$.
7. Use the ensemble via majority vote.

Each stage is deliberately handed the cases the previous stage found hard: $D_2$ is
half errors by construction, and $D_3$ is nothing but disagreements.

*** Adaptive boosting (AdaBoost)

Often called AdaBoost; the version in the deck is also referred to as
AdaBoost.M1. It often uses decision trees (or decision stumps) as
base learners, but this is not required. The ensemble chooses a class as

\[
  \mathit{class} = \arg\max_{c} \sum_{i=1}^{m} \log\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta_i}\right) \rho_i(x)
\]

so each learner's vote is weighted by $\log(1/\beta_i)$, where $\beta_i$ is derived
from learner $i$'s weighted error. A learner with low error gets a large weight; one
near chance gets a weight near zero.

*** Advanced boosting

More advanced boosting algorithms have been developed in recent years:

| Gradient Boosting | Train learners correlated with the negative gradient of the loss function with respect to the entire ensemble. |
| XGBoost           | Gradient boosting with regularization added to the loss function.                                            |
| LightGBM          | Gradient boosting with one-sided sampling (reduces training examples needed) and feature bundling (reduces features to be evaluated). |
| CatBoost          | Omits the sample being used to train when estimating the gradient, similar to a "jackknife" sample.          |

The deck focuses on basic gradient boosting as the common core.

*** Gradient boosting

Core idea: develop base learners that are highly correlated with the gradient of the loss function for the entire ensemble.

The goal is to learn an approximation $\hat{F}(x)$ of an underlying target function
$F(x)$ by minimizing a loss function $L(y, F(x))$ and constructing a weighted sum of
functions:

\[
  \hat{F}_m(x) = \hat{F}_{m-1}(x) + \gamma_m f_m(x)
\]

where $\gamma_m$ is the weight of the \(m\)-th function $f_m(x)$. We assume

\[
  \hat{F}_0(x) = \arg\min_{f} \sum_{i=1}^{n} L(y_i, f(x_i))
\]

Each successive base learner finds

\[
  \gamma_m f_m(x) = \arg\min_{\gamma, f} \sum_{i=1}^{n}
     L\big(y_i,\; \hat{F}_{m-1}(x_i) + \gamma f(x_i)\big)
\]

Significantly, the first learner trains on the original data and subsequent learners
train on the residuals
of the ensemble so far:

\[
  r_{m,i} = -\left[\frac{\partial L(y_i, \hat{F}(x_i))}{\partial \hat{F}(x_i)}\right]_{\hat{F}(x) = \hat{F}_{m-1}(x)}
\]

With squared-error loss that derivative is just $y_i - \hat{F}_{m-1}(x_i)$, which is
why "fit the next tree to the residuals" and "follow the negative gradient" are the
same instruction here.

** Stacking

So far every method has assumed the models in the ensemble are of the /same/ type.
Stacking allows different types of models to be combined. The primary advantage is
that it lets models with different inductive biases contribute to the overall
prediction based on their individual strengths. A meta-learner
is used
to combine the predictions:

: Training Data -> [Base Learner 1 ... Base Learner m] -> Prediction Data -> Meta Learner -> Combined Prediction

*** Learning for stacking

- Base learners are referred to as Level 0
  models.
- The meta-learner results in a Level 1
  model, whose purpose is to
  combine predictions.
- The data set for the Level 1 model consists of (predictions, target) pairs.

So the two levels train on differently shaped data:

- *Level 0*: the learning algorithm $k$ is trained on
  $\{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{n}$ and produces $f_k(x)$.
- *Level 1*: the meta learner is trained on
  $\{(f_{i,k},\, y_{i,k})\}_{i=1,k=1}^{(n,m)}$ and produces $F(x)$.

The Level 1 training set is the base learners' /outputs/, not the original features
--- which is why the meta-learner can be simple even when the base learners are not.

[The stacking diagram (p39) is a slide image --- see the PDF.]

*** Types of models

- Different types of models are used for the Level 0 models --- e.g. SVM, decision
  tree, neural network.
- For classification, the meta-model can be anything but is often logistic
  regression. If a linear classifier is insufficient, move to a more sophisticated
  model.
- For regression, the meta-model can be anything but is often linear regression.
  Likewise, if linear regression is insufficient, move to something more
  sophisticated.

** Overfitting

The conventional wisdom is that all ensemble methods are robust with respect to
overfitting, and regularization is based upon diversity among members of the ensemble.
Where that diversity comes from differs by method:

| Bagging           | Diversity comes from the individual data sets.                                      |
| Boosting          | Diversity comes from re-weighting the data and then weighting the base learners.    |
| Gradient Boosting | Diversity comes from focusing on combining predictions focused on the residuals.    |
| Stacking          | Diversity comes from the different inductive biases of the base learners.           |

Which makes diversity the single thread running through the whole module: it is the
mechanism behind each method, the thing ensemble selection trades against size, and
the quantity Yule's Q and the entropy measure were defined to score.

* Clustering and Association Analysis

The last block of the deck returns to unsupervised learning, picking up from the

** Agglomerative vs divisive

Hierarchical clustering comes in two directions, and splits again by how many
features a split considers at a time:

|             | Agglomerative (bottom-up, merge)                   | Divisive (top-down, split)              |
| Polythetic  | HAC --- merges on a distance over all features     | Feature building                        |
| Monothetic  |                                                    | Association analysis --- splits on one attribute |

Polythetic
methods consider all features at once; monothetic
methods work one attribute at a time. HAC is the
agglomerative polythetic case and is covered next; association analysis is the
monothetic divisive case.

** Linkage rules

HAC needs a rule for the distance between two /clusters/, given a distance $\delta$
between points. Each rule merges the pair $C_i, C_j$ minimizing $D(C_i, C_j)$, and
they differ only in how a cluster-to-cluster distance is formed:

- *Single linkage* --- the nearest pair of points:

\[
  D(C_i, C_j) = \min_{x \in C_i,\, z \in C_j} \delta(x, z)
\]

- *Complete linkage* --- the farthest pair of points:

\[
  D(C_i, C_j) = \max_{x \in C_i,\, z \in C_j} \delta(x, z)
\]

- *Average linkage* --- the mean over all cross-cluster pairs:

\[
  D(C_i, C_j) = \frac{1}{m_i m_j} \sum_{x \in C_i,\, z \in C_j} \delta(x, z)
\]

- *Ward's method* --- merge the pair that increases the within-cluster sum of squares
  the least:

\[
  D(C_i, C_j) = \mathit{ESS}_{ij} - \mathit{ESS}_{i} - \mathit{ESS}_{j},
  \qquad
  \mathit{ESS}_{k} = \sum_{x \in C_k} \| x - \bar{x}_k \|^2
\]

The practical difference: single linkage will chain clusters together through a
sequence of near neighbours, complete linkage insists on compactness and so resists
chaining, average linkage sits between them, and Ward's method optimizes the same
criterion k-means does but does it greedily and hierarchically.

** Association analysis

The monothetic divisive case: split on the single attribute that best separates the
data. For binary attributes, let $F_{ij}$ be the \(j\)-th attribute of the \(i\)-th data
point and $F_{ik}$ the \(k\)-th attribute of the same point. Counting co-occurrences
over the $n$ data points gives a $2 \times 2$ table for each attribute pair
$(j, k)$:

\begin{align*}
a &= \sum_{i=1}^{n} F_{ij} \wedge F_{ik} \\
b &= \sum_{i=1}^{n} (1 - F_{ij}) \wedge F_{ik} \\
c &= \sum_{i=1}^{n} F_{ij} \wedge (1 - F_{ik}) \\
d &= \sum_{i=1}^{n} (1 - F_{ij}) \wedge (1 - F_{ik})
\end{align*}

The association strength between attributes $j$ and $k$ is then normalized as

\[
  \phi_{jk} = \frac{ad - bc}{\sqrt{(a + b)(a + c)(b + d)(c + d)}}
\]

and the attribute to split on is the one with the greatest total association with
the rest:

\[
  F_{\mathit{split}} = \arg\max_{j} \sum_{k=1}^{d} \phi_{jk}
\]

Non-binary attributes have to be converted to binary first, then normalized. Note
that $\phi$ is the familiar correlation coefficient specialised to two binary
variables --- so "split on the most associated attribute" is "split on the attribute
that carries the most information about the others".

** Density-based clustering

The alternative to both hierarchical and centroid methods: define clusters as
regions of high point density, separated by regions of low density. This finds
clusters of arbitrary shape --- the thing k-means cannot do, since it can only
produce convex, roughly spherical cells --- and it does not require the number of
clusters up front.

*** Point categorization

Two parameters: a radius $\varepsilon$ and a minimum count $\mathit{minPts}$. Every
point falls into one of three categories by how many points lie within
$\varepsilon$ of it:

- Core point
  --- at least $\mathit{minPts}$ points within
  $\varepsilon$ (counting itself). It is in the interior of a dense region.
- Border point
  --- fewer than $\mathit{minPts}$ neighbours, but within
  $\varepsilon$ of a core point. It is on the edge of a cluster.
- Noise point
  --- neither. It belongs to no cluster.

*** DBSCAN

Given that categorization, the algorithm is short: form clusters as the connected
components of the core points under "within $\varepsilon$ of each other", then
attach each border point to a cluster of an adjacent core point. Noise points are
left unassigned --- which is the other thing k-means cannot do, since k-means must
assign every point to some centroid.

The parameters are the catch: $\varepsilon$ and $\mathit{minPts}$ have to be chosen,
and a single $\varepsilon$ cannot serve clusters of substantially different
densities.

[The DBSCAN slides (p50--52) and two further worked examples (p53--54) are slide
images --- read them off the PDF, since the examples are what make the
core/border/noise distinction concrete.]
