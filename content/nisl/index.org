* 1 Intro to Probability Theory

** 1.1.2 Basic Concepts in Probability Theory

In Bayesian formalism, belief measures obey three basic axioms of probability calculus:

1. \(0 \leq P(A) \leq 1\)

2. \(P(\text{sure proposition}) = 1\)

3. \(P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B) \text{ if } A \text{ and } B \text{ are mutually exclusive} \)

*** Marginalizing Probabilities

*Law of Total Probability*: In a joint probability, you can sum over the states of an event to /marginalize/ that probability. 
For example, if we are calculating $P(A)$ from $P\left( A, B \right)$, and $B$ has binary states, we can marginalize over the states of $B$:


\[
  P(A) = P\left( A, B \right) + P\left( A, \neg B \right) \text{ where } P\left( A,B \right) \text{ is short for } P\left( A \wedge B \right)
\]

If B had more than two states, we can some over all of them to marginalize $B$ of the joint probability:

\[
  P\left( A \right) = \sum_{i=1}^{} P\left( A, B_{i} \right)
\]

The process of summing up probabilities all over $B_{i}$ is called /Marginalizing/ over $B$; and the resulting probability $P\left( A \right)$, is called the /marginal/ probability of $A$.

*** Conditional Probability

The Conditional probabilities entails a Causality between two events. The probability distribution of one event is directly effected by the state of another event. If the state of an event $A$ doesn't change the probability distribution of event $B$, then we say that $A$ and $B$ are /independent/.

The conditional probability represented as a joint event is:

\[
  P\left( A \mid B \right)  = \frac{P\left( A,B \right)}{P\left( B \right)}
\]

Which is just dividing out $B$'s probability from the joint probability.

Visa versa, we can flip the equation around to calculate the joint probability of two events:
\[
  P\left( A,B \right) = P\left( A \mid B \right) P\left( B \right)
\]


*** Chain Rule

A large joint probability with more than two events can be written by a /chain/ of conditional probabilities:

\[
  P\left( E_1, E_{2}, \ldots, E_n \right) = P\left( E_{n} \mid E_{n-1}, \ldots, E_2, E_1 \right) \ldots P\left( E_2 \mid E_1 \right) P\left( E_1 \right)
\]



*** Bayes' Rule

The inversion formula for a conditional probability is the heart of Bayesian Probability. 

\[
  P\left( H \mid e \right) = \frac{P\left( e \mid H \right) P\left( H \right)}{P\left( e \right)} 
\]

| Char                       | Def                     |
| H                          | Hypothesis              |
| e                          | evidence                |
| $P\left( H \right)$        | Previous belief (prior) |
| $P\left( e \mid H \right)$ | likelihood              |
| $P\left( H \mid e \right)$ | Posterior               |

*** Probabilistic Model

The Bayesian perspective is interesting because we can empirically verify relationships between English expressions.

The /probabilistic model/ (aka /probability space/) is an encoding of information that lets us compute the probability of every well-informed sentence $S$ with the three basic axioms at the beginning of this section. 

** 1.1.3 Combining Predictive and Diagnostic Supports


The essence of Bayes' Rule can be showed as /odds/ and /likelihood ratio/ parameters by dividing Baye's rule by the complementary form for $P\left( \neg H \mid e \right)$, we get:

\[
  \frac{P\left( H \mid e \right)}{P\left( \neg H \mid e \right)} = \frac{P\left( e \mid H \right)}{P\left( e \mid \neg H \right)} \frac{P\left( H \right)}{P\left( \neg H \right)}
\]

/Prior Odds/ on H:

\[
  O(H) = \frac{P(H)}{P(\neg H)} = \frac{P(H)}{1-P(H)}
\]

/likelihood Ratio/:

\[
  L(e \mid H) = \frac{P(e \mid H)}{P(e \mid \neg H)}
\]

/Posterior Odds/:

\[
  O(H \mid e) = \frac{P(H \mid e)}{P(\neg H \mid e)}
\]

is given by the product:

\[
  O(H \mid e) = L(e \mid H) O(H)
\]


| char   | Def                                                                                                             |
| $O(H)$ | Prior odds: Measure the Predictive or prospective support according to $H$ with the background knowledge alone. |
|  $L(e \mid H)$      | Likelihood ratio: Represents the /diagnostic/ or /retrospective/ support given to $H$ given by the evidence actually observed. |

*** Probability vs. Odds vs. Likelihood

These three quantities are easy to conflate, but they answer different questions.

*Probability* $P(H \mid e)$: how likely a hypothesis is, given the evidence. It is /normalized/ — it lives in $[0,1]$ and the states of $H$ sum to 1. This is what Bayes' Rule computes (the posterior). It answers /"how much should I believe H?"/

*Odds* $O(H) = \frac{P(H)}{P(\neg H)} = \frac{P(H)}{1 - P(H)}$: a re-expression of a probability as the ratio of "it's true" to "it's false." It is just probability in a different coordinate system — running $[0, \infty)$ instead of $[0,1]$. Odds are convenient because updating becomes a simple product: $O(H \mid e) = L(e \mid H)\, O(H)$.

*Likelihood* $P(e \mid H)$: the /reversed/ conditional — how well a hypothesis predicts the evidence actually observed. Here $H$ is held fixed and $e$ varies, so as a function of $H$ it is /not/ a probability distribution; it need not sum to 1. It answers /"if H were true, how expected is this data?"/

| Quantity                   | What varies    | Normalized to 1?      | Question it answers          |
| Probability $P(H \mid e)$  | the hypothesis | yes                   | How much do I believe H?     |
| Odds $O(H)$                | (same as prob) | no — ranges $[0,\infty)$ | H vs. not-H, as a ratio      |
| Likelihood $P(e \mid H)$   | the evidence   | no                    | How well does H predict data? |

In short: /probability/ measures belief in a hypothesis, /odds/ re-package that belief as a ratio (making Bayesian updates multiplicative), and /likelihood/ measures how well a hypothesis predicts the data — the same conditional as probability but with the roles of $H$ and $e$ swapped, which is why it isn't a distribution.



** 1.1.4 Random Variables and Expectations

- /Variable/: attribute; takes one of several possible outcomes. *Capital letters* ($X, Y, Z$) are variables names
- /Value/: the specific outcome. *Lowercase letters* ($x, y, z$) represent the specific value of the corresponding variable

- /Partition/: Finite set $V$ of random Variables

Each variable $X \in V$ can take on values from a finite domain $D_{X}$


- /Real/ random variable: When the values of a random variable $X$ consist of real numbers

/Mean/ or /expected value/ of $X$:

\[
  E(X) \triangleq \sum_{x}^{} x P(x)
\]

| Char | Def |
| $\triangleq$     |   "is defined as"  |

/Condition mean/ is the best estimate of $X$ given the observation $Y=y$. /Condition mean/ of $X$ given event $Y=y$:

\[
  E(X \mid y) \triangleq \sum_{x}^{} x P(x \mid y)
\]

/Expectation of any function/ $g$ of $X$:

\[
  E\left[ g(X) \right] \triangleq \sum_{x}^{} g(x) P(x)
\]

For example, here is a function $g(X) = (X - E(X))^{2}$, which is the /variance/ of $X$ denoted $\sigma_{X}^{2}$:

\[
 \sigma_{X}^{2} \triangleq E \left[ (X - E(X))^{2} \right]
\]

/Expectation/ for a two-variable joint probability:

\[
  E \left[ g(X, Y) \right] \triangleq \sum_{x, y}^{} g(x,y) P(x,y)
\]


/Covariance/: how two variables change together. $g(X,Y) = (X-E(X))(Y-E(Y))$

\[
  \sigma_{XY} \triangleq E \left[ \left( X - E(X) \right) \left( Y - E(Y) \right) \right]
\]

/Correlation Coefficient/: normalized covariance:

\[
   \rho_{XY} = \frac{\sigma_{XY}}{\sigma_{X} \sigma_{Y}}
\]

/Regression Coefficient/ (of $X$ and $Y$):

\[
  r_{XY} \triangleq \rho_{XY}\frac{\sigma_{X}}{\sigma_{Y}} = \frac{\sigma_{XY}}{\sigma_{Y}^{2}}
\]


The /conditional/ version of variance, covariance, and correlation coefficient is the same thing, but with $P(x,y \mid z)$ for the expectations.

*** Continuous variables

Expectation and correlation are more often used for continuous variables. Continuous variables are defined with a /density function $f(x)$/:

\[
  P(a \leq X \leq b) = \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx
\]


The continuous version of the probability function is translated with an integral:

\[
  \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x) \, dx \iff \sum_{x}^{} P(X) 
\]

For example, the /expectation/ for a continuous variable is defined as:

\[
  E(X) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x f(x) \, dx
\]

This translations carry over to all of the discrete equations we just talked about.


** 1.1.5 Conditional Independence and Graphoids

/Conditional independence/: if learning the value of $Y$ doesn't give any insight about $X$, once we know $Z$:

\[
  P(x \mid y,z) = P(x \mid z)
\]

Dawid's notation for conditional independence:

\[
  (X \mathrel{⫫} Y \mid Z)_{P} \text{ iff }  P(x \mid y, z) = P(x \mid z)
\]

/Unconditional independence (Marginal independence)/: 

\[
  (X \mathrel{⫫} Y \mid \emptyset) \text{ iff } P(x \mid y) = P(x)
\]

----------------- 

(partial) List of conditional independence relation $(X \mathrel{⫫} Y \mid Z)$, aka /graphoid axioms/. (Use $YW$ as shorthand for $Y \cup W$)

| Word          | Def                                                                          |
| Symmetry      | $(X \mathrel{⫫} Y \mid Z)\implies (Y \mathrel{⫫} X \mid Z)$ --- In any state of knowledge $Z$, if $Y$ tells us nothing new about $X$, then $X$ tells us nothing new about $Y$.                             |
| Decomposition | $(X \mathrel{⫫} YW \mid Z) \implies (X \mathrel{⫫} Y \mid Z)$ --- If two combined items of information are judged irrelevant to $X$, then each separate item is irrelevant as well.                          |
| Weak Union    | $(X \mathrel{⫫} YW \mid Z) \implies (X \mathrel{⫫} Y \mid WZ)$ --- Learning irrelevant information $W$ cannot help the irrelevant information $Y$ become relevant to $X$.                         |
| Contraction   | $(X \mathrel{⫫} Y  \mid Z) \& (X \mathrel{⫫} W \mid ZY) \implies (X \mathrel{⫫} YW \mid Z)$ --- If we judge $W$ irrelevant to $X$ after learning some irrelevant information $Y$, then $W$ must have been irrelevant before we learned $Y$. (Together, weak union and contraction mean irrelevant information does not alter the relevance status of other propositions: what was relevant stays relevant, what was irrelevant stays irrelevant.) |
| Intersection  | $(X \mathrel{⫫} W \mid ZY) \& (X \mathrel{⫫} Y \mid ZW) \implies (X \mathrel{⫫} YW \mid Z)$ --- If $Y$ is irrelevant to $X$ when we know $W$, and $W$ is irrelevant to $X$ when we know $Y$, then neither $W$ nor $Y$ (nor their combination) is relevant to $X$. (only valid in positive probability distributions)|

*** 1.2.2 Bayesian Networks

/Bayesian Network/: Directed graphs or DAGs that represent causal or temporal relationships.

We can calculate a joint probability with the /chain rule/:

\[
  P(x_{1}, \ldots, x_{n}) = \prod_{j}^{} P(x_{j} \mid x_{1}, \ldots, x_{j-1})
\]

This is great, but the product grows exponentially with the number of variables. We can reduce the computation by calculating the conditional probability with only variables that are dependent of each other (cutting out independence lowers the number of conditional probabilities we need to calculate):

\[
  P(x_{j} \mid x_{1}, \ldots, x_{j-1}) = P(x_{j} \mid pa_{j})
\]

The set $PA_{j}$ is the (Markovian) parents of $X_{j}$.

By using the two equations above, we can compute the joint probability more efficiently with:

\[
  P(x_{1}, \ldots, x_{3}) = \prod_{i}^{} P(x_{i} \mid pa_{i})
\]

This product decomposition does not need to be order-specific anymore. 

*Markov Compatibility*:

Markov compatibility is just a fancy way of asking: "does this graph actually match this probability distribution?"

A distribution $P$ is /Markov compatible/ with a DAG $G$ if you can write $P$ using the parent-based product from above:

\[
  P(x_{1}, \ldots, x_{n}) = \prod_{i}^{} P(x_{i} \mid pa_{i})
\]

In other words, the graph is a valid picture of the distribution: every independence the graph claims (each variable depends only on its parents) is really true in $P$. If that decomposition holds, the graph and the distribution "agree" and we say $P$ is compatible with $G$ (equivalently, $G$ /represents/ $P$), or that $P$ is /Markov relative/ to $G$.


*** 1.2.3 /d/-Separation Criterion

#+begin_quote
A path $p$ is said to be /d-separated/ (or blocked) by a set of nodes $Z$ iff 
1. $p$ contains a chain $i \to m \to j$ or a fork $i \leftarrow m \rightarrow j$ such that the middle node $m$ in in $Z$, or 
2. $p$ contains an inverted fork (or collider) $i \rightarrow m \leftarrow j$ such that the middle node $m$ is not in $Z$ and such that no descendent of $m$ is in $Z$.
A set $Z$ is said to /d-separate/ $X$ from $Y$ iff $Z$ blocks every path from a node in $X$ to a node in $Y$
#+end_quote

Intuitively, /d/-separation tells us when information can flow along a path between $X$ and $Y$. A path is either /open/ (information flows ⇒ dependence) or /blocked/ (no flow ⇒ independence). Whether it is open depends only on the /junctions/ it passes through, and there are exactly three:

Notice the collider is the odd one out: for chains and forks, conditioning on the middle node /blocks/ the path, but for a collider, conditioning on the middle node (or one of its descendants) /opens/ it. So a path is blocked iff it contains a non-collider that is /in/ $Z$, or a collider that is /not/ in $Z$ and has no descendant in $Z$.

$Z$ /d-separates/ $X$ from $Y$ when it blocks /every/ path between them — which guarantees $X \mathrel{⫫} Y \mid Z$ in every distribution compatible with the graph.

In short: conditioning on the middle node $M$ makes the /chain/ and the /fork/ conditionally /independent/, whereas it makes the /collider/ conditionally /dependent/.
