* Scheduling

** Scheduling

*** What is the aim of processor scheduling?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: To assign processes to be executed by the processor over time in a way that meets system objectives such as response time, throughput, and processor efficiency.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 Process Management › Scheduling.
:Q_UNIT: sched
:END:

** Types of Scheduling

*** Define long-term scheduling.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: The decision to add to the pool of processes to be executed, i.e. which programs are admitted to the system.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 Process Management › Types of Scheduling. It drives the New to Ready transition.
:Q_UNIT: sched
:END:

*** Define medium-term scheduling.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: The decision to add to the number of processes partially or fully in main memory; it is part of the swapping function.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 Process Management › Types of Scheduling › Medium-Term Scheduling. It drives the suspend transitions.
:Q_UNIT: sched
:END:

*** Define short-term scheduling.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: The decision as to which available (ready) process will be executed by the processor next.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 Process Management › Types of Scheduling › Short-Term Scheduling. Also called the dispatcher; it drives Ready to Running.
:Q_UNIT: sched
:END:

*** Define I/O scheduling.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: The decision as to which process's pending I/O request is handled by an available I/O device.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 Process Management › Types of Scheduling.
:Q_UNIT: sched
:END:

*** Which process-state transition does each of long-, medium-, and short-term scheduling drive?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Long-term drives New to Ready, medium-term drives the suspend transitions (Ready/Suspend and Blocked/Suspend), and short-term drives Ready to Running.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 Process Management › Types of Scheduling (Figure 9.1).
:Q_UNIT: sched
:END:

*** The long-term scheduler controls the degree of __________.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: fill
:Q_ANSWER: multiprogramming
:Q_ACCEPT: multi-programming
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 Process Management › Long-Term Scheduling. The more processes created, the smaller the percentage of time each gets to execute.
:Q_UNIT: sched
:END:

*** What two decisions does the long-term scheduler make?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: When the OS can take on one or more additional processes, and which jobs to accept and turn into processes.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 Process Management › Long-Term Scheduling. "When" depends on the degree of multiprogramming; "which" can be FCFS or by priority, expected execution time, and I/O requirements.
:Q_UNIT: sched
:END:

*** How does an interactive time-sharing system handle new users differently from a batch long-term scheduler?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Users are not queued and kept waiting; the OS accepts all authorized users until the system is saturated.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 Process Management › Long-Term Scheduling.
:Q_UNIT: sched
:END:

*** Name the four kinds of events that invoke the short-term scheduler.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Clock interrupts, I/O interrupts, operating system calls, and signals (e.g. semaphores).
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 Process Management › Short-Term Scheduling.
:Q_UNIT: sched
:END:

*** Which scheduler executes most frequently?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: mc
:Q_OPTIONS: Short-term (dispatcher) | Medium-term | Long-term | I/O
:Q_ANSWER: Short-term (dispatcher)
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 Process Management › Short-Term Scheduling. It makes the fine-grained decision of which process runs next.
:Q_UNIT: sched
:END:

** Short-Term Scheduling Criteria

*** What is the difference between user-oriented and system-oriented scheduling criteria?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: User-oriented criteria describe behavior as perceived by the individual user or process, while system-oriented criteria describe effective and efficient utilization of the processor.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 Process Management › Short-Term Scheduling Criteria.
:Q_UNIT: sched
:END:

*** Define turnaround time.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: The interval between the submission of a process and its completion.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 Process Management › User-oriented, performance-related. Written \(T_r\).
:Q_UNIT: sched
:END:

*** Define response time.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: For an interactive process, the time from submission of a request until the response begins to be received.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 Process Management › User-oriented, performance-related.
:Q_UNIT: sched
:END:

*** Define predictability as a scheduling criterion.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: A given job should run in about the same amount of time and at about the same cost regardless of the load on the system.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 Process Management › User-oriented, non-performance-related.
:Q_UNIT: sched
:END:

*** Define throughput.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: The number of processes completed per unit of time.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 Process Management › System-oriented, performance-related.
:Q_UNIT: sched
:END:

*** Classify each criterion: turnaround time, throughput, predictability, fairness.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Turnaround time is user-oriented performance; throughput is system-oriented performance; predictability is user-oriented non-performance; fairness is system-oriented non-performance.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 Process Management › Short-Term Scheduling Criteria (the 2 x 2 table).
:Q_UNIT: sched
:END:

*** System-oriented criteria are generally of minor importance on single-user systems.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: tf
:Q_ANSWER: True
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 Process Management › Short-Term Scheduling Criteria. User-oriented criteria matter on virtually all systems.
:Q_UNIT: sched
:END:

** Priority-Based Scheduling

*** What is the problem with a pure priority scheduling scheme, and how is it fixed?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Lower-priority processes can starve; the fix is to make priority a function of age and execution history so a waiting process drifts upward.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 Process Management › Priority-Based Scheduling.
:Q_UNIT: sched
:END:

** Scheduling Policies

*** What is a scheduling policy's selection function?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: The rule that determines which process, among the ready processes, is selected next, written in terms of w, e, and s.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 Process Management › Scheduling Policies. w = time waiting in the system so far, e = time in execution so far, s = total service time.
:Q_UNIT: sched
:END:

*** What is the difference between nonpreemptive and preemptive decision modes?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Nonpreemptive: a running process continues until it terminates or blocks itself; preemptive: the OS may interrupt the running process and move it to the ready state.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 Process Management › Scheduling Policies.
:Q_UNIT: sched
:END:

*** Give one disadvantage and one advantage of preemptive scheduling.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Disadvantage: more overhead, since every preemption is a context switch; advantage: better service to the process population because no single process can monopolize the processor.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 Process Management › Nonpreemptive vs preemptive.
:Q_UNIT: sched
:END:

*** Give the selection function and decision mode of each policy: FCFS, RR, SPN, SRT, HRRN.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: FCFS max w, nonpreemptive; RR constant, preemptive at quantum q; SPN min s, nonpreemptive; SRT min(s - e), preemptive; HRRN max (w + s)/s, nonpreemptive.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 Process Management › Scheduling Policies (each policy's subsection). Feedback is preemptive at each quantum and uses execution history.
:Q_STEPS: FCFS :: max w, nonpreemptive | RR :: constant, preemptive (quantum q) | SPN :: min s, nonpreemptive | SRT :: min(s - e), preemptive | HRRN :: max (w+s)/s, nonpreemptive | Feedback :: queue level by history, preemptive (quantum)
:Q_UNIT: sched
:END:

** First-Come-First-Served (FCFS)

*** Name the two biases of FCFS.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: It performs much better for long processes than short ones, and it favors processor-bound processes over I/O-bound ones.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 Process Management › First-Come-First-Served (FCFS).
:Q_UNIT: sched
:END:

*** Why does FCFS leave I/O devices idle when a CPU-bound process is running?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: The short I/O-bound processes are stuck in the ready queue behind the long CPU-bound process, so they cannot issue their I/O requests.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 Process Management › First-Come-First-Served (FCFS). The deck's P1 (100 ms CPU) vs P2, P3 (2 ms CPU then I/O) example.
:Q_UNIT: sched
:END:

** Round Robin

*** Why is round robin also called time slicing?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Preemption is based on a clock: at each periodic clock interrupt the running process goes to the back of the ready queue and the next one runs.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 Process Management › Round Robin.
:Q_UNIT: sched
:END:

*** What is the trade-off in choosing the round-robin quantum length?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: A short quantum moves short processes through quickly but adds clock-interrupt and scheduling overhead, so the quantum should be slightly greater than a typical interaction.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 Process Management › Round Robin.
:Q_UNIT: sched
:END:

*** What is round robin's drawback with I/O-bound processes, and what is the fix?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: I/O-bound processes block before using their full quantum, so they get less processor time than CPU-bound ones; virtual round robin gives them a preferential auxiliary queue.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 Process Management › Round Robin.
:Q_UNIT: sched
:END:

** Shortest Process Next (SPN)

*** What is the main difficulty in implementing SPN?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: The required processing time of each process must be known or estimated, from stored statistics or from the user/programmer.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 Process Management › Shortest Process Next (SPN).
:Q_UNIT: sched
:END:

*** Name three costs of SPN.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Possible starvation of longer processes, increased variability of response time (less predictable), and unsuitability for time-sharing systems.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 Process Management › Shortest Process Next (SPN).
:Q_UNIT: sched
:END:

** Shortest Remaining Time (SRT)

*** What is the difference between SPN and SRT?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: SRT is the preemptive version of SPN: it picks the shortest expected remaining time and re-decides whenever a new process arrives.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 Process Management › Shortest Remaining Time (SRT).
:Q_UNIT: sched
:END:

*** Why does SRT have lower overhead than round robin?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: It generates no additional interrupts; the dispatcher runs only when a new process arrives (or one finishes), not on every clock tick.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 Process Management › Shortest Remaining Time (SRT).
:Q_UNIT: sched
:END:

** Highest Response Ratio Next (HRRN)

*** Write the HRRN response ratio and explain why HRRN avoids starvation.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: R = (w + s)/s; a short s makes R large, but waiting increases w and therefore R, so a long process eventually gets past shorter jobs.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 Process Management › Highest Response Ratio Next (HRRN).
:Q_UNIT: sched
:END:

** Multilevel Feedback

*** When is multilevel feedback used instead of SPN, SRT, or HRRN?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: When there is no indication of the relative length of processes, since SPN, SRT, and HRRN all need the service time s.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 Process Management › Multilevel Feedback.
:Q_UNIT: sched
:END:

*** Describe how a process moves through the multilevel feedback queues.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: It enters RQ0, drops one queue lower each time it is preempted, the lowest queue is FCFS, and the scheduler always serves the highest non-empty queue.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 Process Management › Multilevel Feedback.
:Q_UNIT: sched
:END:

*** What is the main cost of multilevel feedback, and how does a quantum of 2^i help?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Turnaround time for long processes stretches out because they keep sinking while new arrivals enter above them; a quantum that grows with depth (2^i at level i) softens this.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 Process Management › Multilevel Feedback.
:Q_UNIT: sched
:END:

** Windows priority levels

*** What are the Windows thread priority ranges?
:PROPERTIES:

** Dining Philosophers Problem

*** State the two requirements of the dining philosophers problem.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: No two philosophers can use the same fork at the same time (mutual exclusion), and no philosopher may starve (avoid deadlock and starvation).
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 › Theoretical Problems in Concurrency › Dining Philosophers Problem.
:Q_UNIT: cprob
:END:

*** How can the simple dining philosophers solution (each philosopher picks up the left fork, then the right) deadlock?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: If all five pick up their left fork at the same time, each waits forever for a right fork that a neighbor holds.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 › Dining Philosophers Problem › First attempt: one semaphore per fork.
:Q_UNIT: cprob
:END:

*** Give two ways to prevent deadlock in the dining philosophers problem.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Add five more forks, or add a room attendant who lets only 4 philosophers into the room at a time.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 › Dining Philosophers Problem › What can you do?
:Q_UNIT: cprob
:END:

*** In the dining philosophers problem, why does letting at most four philosophers sit at the table at once prevent deadlock?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: With at most four philosophers competing for five forks, at least one of them can always get both forks, eat, and release them.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 › Dining Philosophers Problem › Second attempt: the room semaphore.
:Q_UNIT: cprob
:END:

*** How can a monitor solve the dining philosophers problem?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Philosophers get and release forks through monitor procedures (only one inside at a time); a philosopher waits on a fork's condition variable if it is taken, and releasing a fork signals a philosopher waiting for it, or marks it free if no one is.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 › Dining Philosophers Problem › Solution using a monitor.
:Q_UNIT: cprob
:END:

** Producer/Consumer Problem

*** State the producer/consumer problem.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Producers place data in a buffer and a consumer removes items one at a time, with only one accessing the buffer at once; the producer must not add to a full buffer and the consumer must not take from an empty one.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 › Theoretical Problems in Concurrency › Producer/Consumer Problem.
:Q_UNIT: cprob
:END:

*** In a producer/consumer solution using two binary semaphores, what job does each semaphore do?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: One gives mutual exclusion on the buffer; the other makes the consumer wait when there is nothing to consume.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 › Producer/Consumer Problem › Initial solution: two binary semaphores.
:Q_UNIT: cprob
:END:

*** Why is this binary-semaphore producer/consumer solution incorrect?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: The consumer tests if (n==0) outside the critical section, so the producer can change n in between; a leftover delay signal then lets the consumer take from an empty buffer and n reaches -1.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 › Producer/Consumer Problem › Why it fails: the trace (Stallings Figure 5.12).
:Q_DATA: void consumer() { | . semWaitB(delay); | . while (true) { | . . semWaitB(s); take(); n--; semSignalB(s); | . . consume(); | . . if (n==0) semWaitB(delay); | . } | }
:Q_STEPS: step 9: consumer leaves CS with n = 0 but has not tested n yet | steps 10-13: producer appends, n = 1, signals delay (delay = 1) | step 14: consumer tests n==0, n is 1, so it does not wait | steps 15-17: consumer takes the item, n = 0 | step 18: consumer tests n==0, semWaitB(delay) uses the stale signal and does not block | steps 19-21: consumer takes from an empty buffer, n = -1
:Q_UNIT: cprob
:END:

*** In a binary-semaphore producer/consumer solution, why should the consumer test a copy of the item count taken inside the critical section?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Testing the shared count after leaving the critical section lets the producer change it in between; a copy taken while holding the lock reflects what the consumer actually saw.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 › Producer/Consumer Problem › Fix 1: copy n inside the critical section.
:Q_UNIT: cprob
:END:

*** How do counting semaphores solve the producer/consumer problem with an unbounded buffer?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: One semaphore counts the items (the producer signals it after adding, the consumer waits on it before taking), and a second gives mutual exclusion on the buffer.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 › Producer/Consumer Problem › Fix 2: general (counting) semaphores (Stallings Figure 5.14).
:Q_STEPS: semaphore n = 0, s = 1; | producer: while (true) { produce(); semWait(s); append(); semSignal(s); semSignal(n); } | consumer: while (true) { semWait(n); semWait(s); take(); semSignal(s); consume(); }
:Q_UNIT: cprob
:END:

*** In a semaphore producer/consumer solution, does the order in which the producer signals the buffer mutex and the item count matter?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: No: correctness is not affected, because the consumer still has to acquire the buffer mutex before touching the buffer.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 › Producer/Consumer Problem › Questions.
:Q_UNIT: cprob
:END:

*** In a semaphore producer/consumer solution, why must the consumer wait on the item count before acquiring the buffer mutex?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Otherwise it can hold the mutex while the buffer is empty and block waiting for an item, so the producer can never get in to add one: deadlock.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 › Producer/Consumer Problem › Questions.
:Q_UNIT: cprob
:END:

*** In the bounded-buffer producer/consumer solution, what does each of the three semaphores do, and what is its initial value?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: A mutex on the buffer (starts at 1), a count of filled slots the consumer waits on (starts at 0), and a count of empty slots the producer waits on (starts at the buffer size).
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 › Producer/Consumer Problem › Bounded buffer (Stallings Figure 5.16).
:Q_UNIT: cprob
:END:

*** Write the bounded-buffer producer and consumer.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: The producer waits on e then s, appends, and signals s then n; the consumer waits on n then s, takes, and signals s then e.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 › Producer/Consumer Problem › Bounded buffer (Stallings Figure 5.16).
:Q_STEPS: semaphore s = 1, n = 0, e = sizeofbuffer; | producer: while (true) { produce(); semWait(e); semWait(s); append(); semSignal(s); semSignal(n); } | consumer: while (true) { semWait(n); semWait(s); take(); semSignal(s); semSignal(e); consume(); }
:Q_UNIT: cprob
:END:

*** State the three conditions of the readers/writers problem.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Any number of readers may read at once; only one writer at a time may write; if a writer is writing, no reader may read.

*** In the readers-have-priority readers/writers solution, what two things need mutual exclusion?

:Q_UNIT: cprob
:END:

*** In the readers-have-priority readers/writers solution, how do readers keep writers out?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short

:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 › Readers/Writers Problem › Solution 1: readers have priority.
:Q_UNIT: cprob
:END:

*** What is the weakness of the readers-have-priority solution?

:Q_TYPE: short

*** What is the idea behind the writers-have-priority solution?

:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 › Readers/Writers Problem › Solution 2: writers have priority.
:Q_UNIT: cprob
:END:

*** What is the weakness of the writers-have-priority solution?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short

:Q_UNIT: cprob
:END:

*** How can readers/writers be solved so that neither readers nor writers starve?

:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: wsem and rsem are set, and writers queue on wsem.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 › Readers/Writers Problem › Solution 2 (state of the process queues table).

:PROPERTIES:

** How to Deal with Deadlocks?

*** Name the three strategies for dealing with deadlock and give the idea of each.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Prevention disallows one of the conditions; avoidance refuses any request that might lead to deadlock; detection grants requests when possible and periodically checks for deadlock, then recovers.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 › Concurrency: Deadlock and Starvation › How to Deal with Deadlocks?
:Q_UNIT: dead
:END:

*** What is the difference between deadlock prevention and deadlock avoidance?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Prevention designs the system so one of the necessary conditions can never occur; avoidance dynamically checks each request and grants it only if the resulting state stays safe.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 › How to Deal with Deadlocks? › Prevention vs avoidance.
:Q_UNIT: dead
:END:

** Deadlock Prevention

*** What is the difference between indirect and direct deadlock prevention?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Indirect prevents one of the three necessary conditions (mutual exclusion, hold-and-wait, no preemption); direct prevents circular wait.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 › Concurrency: Deadlock and Starvation › Deadlock Prevention.
:Q_UNIT: dead
:END:

*** Why can't mutual exclusion generally be designed away to prevent deadlock?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: If access to a resource requires mutual exclusion, the OS must support it; even read-shared files still need exclusive access for writes.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 › Deadlock Prevention › Condition by condition.
:Q_UNIT: dead
:END:

*** How is hold-and-wait prevented?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Require each process to request all its resources at one time, blocking it until all can be granted together.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 › Deadlock Prevention › Condition by condition.
:Q_UNIT: dead
:END:

*** Give the problems with preventing hold-and-wait.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: A process may wait a long time for all its resources, allocated resources may sit unused for a long time, and a process may not know in advance what it will need.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 › Deadlock Prevention › Condition by condition.
:Q_UNIT: dead
:END:

*** How is the no-preemption condition prevented?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: A process denied a further request must release its resources and request them again, or the OS may preempt a process holding a requested resource and make it release its resources.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 › Deadlock Prevention › Condition by condition.
:Q_UNIT: dead
:END:

*** How is circular wait prevented?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Define a linear ordering of resource types, and have every process request resources in increasing order.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 › Deadlock Prevention › Condition by condition.
:Q_UNIT: dead
:END:

** Deadlock Detection

*** How does the detection strategy differ from prevention in granting requests?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Prevention is conservative and restricts access to resources; detection grants requests whenever possible.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: 2.2 › Concurrency: Deadlock and Starvation › Deadlock Detection.
:Q_UNIT: dead
:END:

*** Write the four steps of the deadlock detection algorithm.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Mark every process with an all-zero allocation row; set W = Available; find an unmarked process whose request row is at most W (stop if none); mark it, add its allocation row to W, and repeat. Unmarked processes at the end are deadlocked.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: Deadlock Avoidance and Detection handout › Deadlock detection algorithm › The algorithm.
:Q_STEPS: 1. Mark each process whose row in A is all zeros | 2. W <- Available | 3. Find unmarked i with Q[i,k] <= W[k] for all k; if none, terminate | 4. Mark i; W[k] <- W[k] + A[i,k] for all k; go to 3 | Deadlock exists if and only if unmarked processes remain
:Q_UNIT: dead
:END:

*** In the deadlock detection algorithm, why is a process that holds no resources marked right away?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: A process that holds no resources cannot participate in a deadlock.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: Deadlock Avoidance and Detection handout › The algorithm, step 1.
:Q_UNIT: dead
:END:

*** Does the order in which eligible rows are picked change the detection algorithm's result?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: No: W only grows, so marking is monotonic and the final set of unmarked processes is the same.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: Deadlock Avoidance and Detection handout › The algorithm.
:Q_UNIT: dead
:END:

*** Which matrix does detection use in place of the claim matrix, and why?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: The request matrix Q: detection asks which processes are blocked on requests they are actually making now, not the worst case they might ever ask for.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: Deadlock Avoidance and Detection handout › Deadlock detection algorithm, and Avoidance vs detection.
:Q_UNIT: dead
:END:

:PROPERTIES:

:PROPERTIES:

:Q_ANSWER: Abort all deadlocked processes; back up each to a checkpoint and restart; successively abort deadlocked processes until the deadlock is gone; successively preempt resources until the deadlock is gone.

:Q_UNIT: dead

** Key synchronization ideas

*** Define a lost wakeup.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: A signal that should let a waiting thread resume is missed (e.g., delivered to the wrong thread or sent when no one was waiting on a condition variable), so an eligible thread stays blocked.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: Assignments › Assignment 2 › Key synchronization ideas.
:Q_UNIT: asg2
:END:

*** Why must a condition-variable wait be wrapped in a while loop rather than an if?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: When the thread resumes, the state may have changed again (or the wakeup was spurious), so it must re-check its predicate before proceeding.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: Assignments › Assignment 2 › Key synchronization ideas.
:Q_UNIT: asg2
:END:

*** What is a time-of-check to time-of-use (TOCTOU) bug?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Checking a condition and acting on it in separate critical sections, so the state can change in between and the action is taken on a condition that is no longer true.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: Assignments › Assignment 2 › Key synchronization ideas.
:Q_UNIT: asg2
:END:

*** How do you avoid a TOCTOU bug?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Do the check and the action in one critical section, holding the lock the whole time, so the state cannot change in between.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: Assignments › Assignment 2 › Key synchronization ideas.
:Q_UNIT: asg2
:END:

*** What is targeted blocking?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: Each waiting thread blocks on the specific condition it needs (e.g., separate odd-available, even-available, and space-available semaphores or condition variables), so it resumes only when it can proceed.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: Assignments › Assignment 2 › Key synchronization ideas.
:Q_UNIT: asg2
:END:

*** Define a wasted wakeup.
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: A thread resumes from a blocking wait only to find it still cannot proceed and must wait again.
:Q_EXPLAIN: Notes: Assignments › Assignment 2 › Key synchronization ideas.
:Q_UNIT: asg2
:END:

*** Why is waking all waiters on one shared condition correct but wasteful?
:PROPERTIES:
:Q_TYPE: short
:Q_ANSWER: No eligible thread is left asleep, but most woken threads find their condition still false and go back to sleep, producing many wasted wakeups.
